# Chapter II — Relevancy of Facts (III): statements by persons who cannot be called as witnesses

This third file in Chapter II covers sections 26 to 33: the family of provisions that let a statement into evidence precisely because its maker cannot be brought to the witness box — dead, untraceable, incapacitated, or unreasonably difficult to produce — followed by the related but distinct provisions on former testimony, business and public records, maps and gazettes, foreign law, and the rule that a statement quoted out of context must be completed for fair understanding. The most important single provision in this file is section 26(a): the dying declaration, one of the oldest and most litigated exceptions in the whole law of evidence. Judgments, opinions of experts, and character evidence follow in later files in this chapter.

## Section 26(a) — dying declarations

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Meena is dead — can her own words still convict her killer?
Meena dies of burn injuries. Before she died, she told a doctor, "Ramesh poured kerosene on me and set me on fire." At Ramesh's trial, the defence advocate, Ritu, argues that Meena's statement should carry little weight — she is dead, she was never cross-examined, and the single greatest safeguard this Act builds around every other kind of testimony is entirely absent here. The prosecutor, Naveen, responds that this is exactly why the Act carves out a specific, named exception for statements about the very transaction that caused a person's own death — not because cross-examination stops mattering, but because a class of statements is treated as reliable enough, on its own distinct logic, to be admitted despite it.
:::

::: story One narrow question, and one widely misunderstood precondition
Section 26(a) makes relevant a statement, by a person now dead, as to the cause of their death or the circumstances of the transaction that resulted in it — but only where the cause of that person's death is itself in question in the proceeding, criminal or civil. The section then does something many students and even practitioners get backwards: it says expressly that it does not matter whether the deceased was, at the time, under an expectation of death. The popular belief that a "dying declaration" requires the maker to have known or believed death was imminent is a misconception this Act's own text directly forecloses — a person who genuinely believed they would recover, and made a calm, matter-of-fact statement about what happened to them, can still give a dying declaration within the meaning of this section, provided the statement relates to the cause or circumstances of the death that in fact followed.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

> **Section 26 — Cases in which statement of relevant fact by person who is dead or cannot be found, etc., is relevant.** Statements, written or verbal, of relevant facts made by a person who is dead, or who cannot be found, or who has become incapable of giving evidence, or whose attendance cannot be procured without an amount of delay or expense which under the circumstances of the case appears to the Court unreasonable, are themselves relevant facts in the following cases, namely:—
>
> (a) when the statement is made by a person as to the [[=cause of his death]], or as to any of the circumstances of the transaction which resulted in his death, in cases in which the cause of that person's death comes into question. Such statements are relevant [[!whether the person who made them was or was not, at the time when they were made, under expectation of death]], and whatever may be the nature of the proceeding in which the cause of his death comes into question;

*Illustration.* The question is, whether A was murdered by B; or A dies of injuries received in a transaction in the course of which she was raped, and the question is whether she was raped by B; or the question is whether A was killed by B under such circumstances that a suit would lie against B by A's widow. Statements made by A as to the cause of his or her death, referring respectively to the murder, the rape, and the actionable wrong under consideration, are relevant facts.

| Statutory phrase | Deconstruction | Practical effect |
|---|---|---|
| "cause of his death, or... circumstances of the transaction which resulted in his death" | **A proximate-connection test**, not a licence for every last thing the deceased said | The statement must bear on the death-causing transaction itself, not merely be something the deceased happened to say before dying. |
| "whether... under expectation of death" is irrelevant | **The single most misunderstood line in this cluster** | A calm, confident statement made by someone who fully expected to survive is not disqualified — the popular "must believe death is imminent" requirement does not exist in this Act's text. |
| "whatever may be the nature of the proceeding" | **Not limited to criminal trials** | The Act's own illustration includes a civil suit for an actionable wrong brought by a widow — a dying declaration can matter well beyond a murder prosecution. |

::: proviso "Circumstances of the transaction" is broad, but not unlimited
A statement genuinely connected to the events immediately surrounding the death — how the injury was inflicted, by whom, and in what immediate sequence — falls comfortably within this section. A statement about matters merely incidental to the deceased's life generally, with no reasonably proximate link to the transaction that caused death, does not qualify merely because it was made by the same dying person in the same conversation.
:::

### In plain English

**Forget "expectation of death" as a requirement — the Act says the opposite.** This is worth over-stating precisely because the misconception is so common: whether the deceased thought they were dying is expressly declared irrelevant to whether the statement qualifies.

**The proximate-connection test is doing real work.** A dying declaration is not "anything the dying person said" — it is specifically their account of the cause or circumstances of the very transaction that killed them, which is why courts examine the actual content of the statement closely rather than treating every recorded utterance from a dying person as automatically covered.

**Reliability is judged after relevance, not instead of it.** Section 26(a) answers whether the statement gets through the door; whether the deceased was lucid, unprompted, and consistent is a separate, weight-focused inquiry a Court makes once the statement is already in.

### Test your instinct

::::instinct "I'll be fine" — and then she wasn't
Before an unexpected complication took her life days later, Meena told a doctor calmly, "I'll be fine, but just so you know, Ramesh poured kerosene on me." She showed no belief that she was dying.

Does the absence of any expectation of death disqualify her statement as a dying declaration?

:::ruling Reveal the ruling
No. Section 26(a) expressly says the statement is relevant whether or not the maker was, at the time, under an expectation of death. Meena's calm confidence that she would recover does not disqualify her account of what happened to her — the popular belief that a dying declaration requires a sense of impending death is not what this Act's text actually requires.
::::

::::instinct The unrelated family remark
In the same conversation with the doctor, Meena also said, "my brother owes me money and should make sure it goes to my daughter's school fees." The prosecution wants to lead this alongside the statement about Ramesh, as part of the same dying declaration.

Is the family-debt remark also relevant under section 26(a)?

:::ruling Reveal the ruling
No. It has no connection — proximate or otherwise — to the cause of Meena's death or the circumstances of the transaction that caused it. Being spoken in the same breath as a genuine dying declaration does not extend section 26(a) to unrelated personal remarks; each part of the statement must independently satisfy the section's own test. The remark about the debt may matter for some entirely different purpose, but not as part of the dying declaration.
::::

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

***Laxman v. State of Maharashtra*, (2002) 6 SCC 710 (Constitution Bench).** The five-judge Bench held that a certificate of the maker's fitness of mind by a medical officer, before a dying declaration is recorded, is a rule of prudence and not a mandatory requirement of law — what matters is that the person recording the statement, whoever they are, is satisfied the maker was in a fit state to make it. This remains the operative authority on how much formality is actually required before a dying declaration can be relied upon, and is frequently the difference between a declaration surviving or failing a defence challenge on procedural grounds alone.

#### Sword and shield

| Used offensively (prosecution / party relying on the declaration) | Used defensively (party resisting it) |
|---|---|
| Lead the statement even without a medical fitness certificate, relying on *Laxman* to show the certificate is prudential, not mandatory — provided some other basis for the maker's lucidity exists. | Challenge the maker's actual state of mind at the time, tutoring or prompting by those present, and internal consistency, since these go to weight and can be fatal even where relevance is not in doubt. |
| Rely on the "whatever may be the nature of the proceeding" language to use a dying declaration in a civil claim, not only a criminal prosecution. | Isolate any part of the statement that lacks proximate connection to the cause or circumstances of death, and object to that part specifically rather than the whole declaration. |

#### The limitation clock

None as a filing deadline, but as a practical matter, a challenge to a dying declaration's reliability (as distinct from its bare relevance) is best raised and developed through cross-examination of the person who recorded it, at the earliest point that witness is available, rather than reserved for closing arguments.

#### Interlocking matrix

| This provision | Interlocks with | Why |
|---|---|---|
| S. 26(a) | Section 19, exception (1), covered in the previous file in this chapter | S. 19's first exception to the self-serving-statement bar is defined by reference to this very section — a maker's own statement can be proved by or on their behalf if it would qualify under s. 26 were they dead. |
| S. 26(a) | The Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023's provisions on recording statements before a Magistrate | The safest practical route to an unchallengeable dying declaration is a Magistrate-recorded statement under that procedure, rather than relying solely on whoever happened to be present. |

#### The authorities

*Khushal Rao v. State of Bombay*, AIR 1958 SC 22 — an early and still-cited authority for the proposition that a dying declaration, if the Court is satisfied it is true and voluntary, can by itself be the sole basis for a conviction, without independent corroboration, and is not inherently a weaker class of evidence merely because the maker could never be cross-examined.

*Pakala Narayan Swami v. Emperor*, AIR 1939 PC 47 (Privy Council) — the same Privy Council decision already noted in this chapter's confessions cluster for its construction of "confession," also construed the equivalent "circumstances of the transaction" phrase under the old Act's dying-declaration provision, holding it requires some reasonably proximate connection to the death rather than reaching every remark the deceased ever made.

#### Strategy and drafting

When recording or relying on a dying declaration, arrange for a fitness-of-mind assessment where practicable even though *Laxman* confirms it is not strictly mandatory — a documented assessment materially strengthens the statement against a defence challenge and costs little to obtain. When resisting one, focus the challenge on the maker's actual state of mind and the presence or absence of tutoring, rather than arguing relevance in the abstract — relevance under section 26(a) is usually the easier battle to lose, and weight is where these cases are actually won or lost.

## Section 26(b) to (h) — the other seven unavailability exceptions

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute A dead accountant's diary entry, and a dead man's admission against his own pocket
Priya sues Suresh for unpaid dues on a supply contract. Her only proof of one disputed delivery is a diary entry made by her accountant, Das, who died last year, recording the delivery in the ordinary course of his bookkeeping duties. Separately, a witness who has since become untraceable, Iqbal, once told a mutual friend "I still owe Suresh forty thousand rupees and never paid him back" — a statement plainly against Iqbal's own pocket. Suresh's advocate calls both statements hearsay from people who can never be cross-examined. Priya's advocate answers that section 26 does not stop at dying declarations — clauses (b) and (c) exist for exactly this: statements made in the ordinary course of business, and statements against the maker's own pecuniary interest, carry their own separate guarantee of trustworthiness precisely because a person routinely does not lie in a business ledger or volunteer a fact that hurts their own wallet.
:::

::: story One shared gate, seven different keys
Every one of clauses (b) through (h) sits behind the same opening gate as clause (a): the maker must be dead, untraceable, incapacitated, or producible only at an unreasonable cost of delay or expense. Section 26 does not relax that threshold clause by clause — it is stated once, at the top of the section, and every lettered clause that follows is simply a different category of statement that becomes relevant once that shared threshold is crossed. What changes from clause to clause is not the unavailability test but the *reason* a particular kind of statement is trusted despite the maker's absence: routine business habit for clause (b), self-harm for clause (c), absence of any motive to falsify for clause (d), and so on through family-relationship statements, transaction documents, and collective utterances.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

> **Section 26 (continued).** Statements... of relevant facts made by a person who is dead, or who cannot be found, or who has become incapable of giving evidence, or whose attendance cannot be procured without an amount of delay or expense which under the circumstances of the case appears to the Court unreasonable, are themselves relevant facts in the following cases, namely:—
>
> (b) when the statement was made by such person [[=in the ordinary course of business]], and in particular when it consists of any entry or memorandum made by him in books kept in the ordinary course of business, or in the discharge of professional duty; or of an acknowledgement written or signed by him of the receipt of money, goods, securities or property of any kind; or of a document used in commerce written or signed by him; or of the date of a letter or other document usually dated, written or signed by him;
>
> (c) when the statement is [[=against the pecuniary or proprietary interest]] of the person making it, or when, if true, it would expose him or would have exposed him to a criminal prosecution or to a suit for damages;
>
> (d) when the statement gives the opinion of any such person, as to the existence of any public right or custom or matter of public or general interest, of the existence of which, if it existed, he would have been likely to be aware, and when such statement was made [[!before any controversy as to such right, custom or matter had arisen]];
>
> (e) when the statement relates to the existence of any relationship by blood, marriage or adoption between persons as to whose relationship by blood, marriage or adoption the person making the statement had special means of knowledge, and when the statement was made [[!before the question in dispute was raised]];
>
> (f) when the statement relates to the existence of any relationship by blood, marriage or adoption between persons deceased, and is made in any will or deed relating to the affairs of the family to which any such deceased person belonged, or in any family pedigree, or upon any tombstone, family portrait or other thing on which such statements are usually made, and when such statement was made [[!before the question in dispute was raised]];
>
> (g) when the statement is contained in any deed, will or other document which relates to any such transaction as is specified in [[=clause (a) of section 11]];
>
> (h) when the statement was made by a number of persons, and expressed [[=feelings or impressions]] on their part relevant to the matter in question.

*Illustrations (selected).* The question is as to the date of A's birth: an entry in the diary of a deceased surgeon, regularly kept in the course of business, stating that on a given day he delivered A's mother of a son, is a relevant fact — clause (b). A sues B for a libel in a painted caricature exposed in a shop window; the remarks of a crowd of spectators on the caricature's similarity and libellous character may be proved — clause (h).

| Clause | Category | Why it is trusted despite the maker's absence |
|---|---|---|
| (b) | Ordinary-course-of-business entries, receipts, dated commercial documents | Routine record-keeping habit gives little reason to fabricate an entry made for no purpose other than the business itself. |
| (c) | Statements against the maker's own pecuniary, proprietary, or penal interest | People do not ordinarily invent facts that expose them to a debt, a lawsuit, or a prosecution. |
| (d) | Opinions on public rights, customs, or matters of general interest, given before any controversy arose | Timing before any dispute existed removes the incentive to shade the opinion toward either side of a fight that had not yet begun. |
| (e), (f) | Statements of blood, marriage, or adoption relationships, made before the dispute arose (or in wills, deeds, pedigrees, tombstones, family portraits) | Special personal knowledge of family relationships, combined with the absence of any live dispute at the time of speaking, substitutes for cross-examination. |
| (g) | Statements in a deed, will, or document relating to a transaction under section 11(a) | The document's connection to the transaction itself is the guarantee, mirroring the transaction-document logic already built into section 11(a). |
| (h) | Collective statements of feeling or impression by a number of persons | A spontaneous, shared reaction by multiple people reduces the risk that any single person's motive to lie drives the content. |

::: proviso Clauses (e) and (f) share a strict timing precondition that clause (a) does not
Unlike the dying declaration in clause (a), both family-relationship clauses require the statement to have been made **before the question in dispute was raised**. A relationship statement manufactured or repeated after litigation is already brewing does not qualify merely because the speaker has since died or become untraceable — the whole trustworthiness rationale for these two clauses depends on the statement predating any incentive to lie about the relationship.
:::

### In plain English

**Section 26 is a list of doors, not one door.** Clause (a) gets the attention because dying declarations are dramatic, but a business ledger entry, a debt admission, or a pedigree statement made by someone now unavailable can walk through a different door in the same section, on its own separate justification.

**Timing is the hidden trap in clauses (d), (e), and (f).** Three of the seven categories build in an express requirement that the statement predate the controversy — miss that precondition and the statement fails no matter how genuinely reliable it otherwise looks, because the whole point of the timing requirement is to rule out statements coloured by an already-existing fight.

**Clause (g) is not a stand-alone category — it borrows section 11(a)'s own test.** A document only qualifies under clause (g) if it relates to a transaction that would already satisfy section 11(a)'s "transaction" test; the two provisions are designed to interlock rather than operate as independent alternatives.

### What changed from the Indian Evidence Act

**Indian Evidence Act, 1872, s. 32(2) to (8) → BSA 2023, s. 26(b) to (h).**

::: info Verify before citing
The widely reported mapping is IEA s. 32(2)→BSA s. 26(b), s. 32(3)→s. 26(c), s. 32(4)→s. 26(d), s. 32(5)→s. 26(e), s. 32(6)→s. 26(f), s. 32(7)→s. 26(g) (with the old Act's internal cross-reference to s. 13 becoming a cross-reference to BSA s. 11(a)), and s. 32(8)→s. 26(h). No verified local copy of the 1872 Act's text is held in `reference/`. Confirm each sub-mapping, and in particular the s. 13→s. 11(a) cross-reference, before relying on it in a filing.

### Test your instinct

::::instinct The pedigree claim raised only after the inheritance fight began
After a will contest breaks out between siblings, one sibling produces a statement supposedly made by their now-deceased aunt, claiming a particular cousin was never actually related by blood to the family. The statement was first repeated by a family friend only after the will contest was already underway, and no earlier record of it exists.

Does this relationship statement qualify under clause (e) or (f)?

:::ruling Reveal the ruling
No. Both clauses require the statement to have been made before the question in dispute was raised. A relationship statement that surfaces only after the fight over the will has already begun does not meet that timing precondition, however sincerely it might be believed — clause (e) and clause (f) are built around removing exactly this kind of after-the-fact incentive to reshape a family's history to fit a live dispute.
::::

::::instinct The dead accountant's delivery entry
Returning to Priya and Suresh: the diary entry made by Priya's late accountant, Das, recorded the disputed delivery as part of his routine, day-to-day bookkeeping duties, with no litigation of any kind on the horizon at the time he wrote it.

Is this entry relevant under section 26?

:::ruling Reveal the ruling
Yes, under clause (b). It is an entry made by a person now dead, in the ordinary course of business, in a book he routinely kept as part of his professional duties. Clause (b) does not require the entry to have predated any dispute the way clauses (e) and (f) do — the trustworthiness guarantee here comes from the routine, businesslike nature of the record-keeping itself, not from timing relative to a controversy.
::::

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

No single case is promoted here. Clauses (b) through (h) are a categorical, definitional cluster — the kind of provision that is litigated through its own text and illustrations far more often than through a landmark ratio, and forcing a promotion here would overstate how much any one judgment actually drives outcomes across seven quite different categories.

#### Sword and shield

| Used offensively (party relying on the statement) | Used defensively (party resisting it) |
|---|---|
| Identify the single most specific clause that fits — a business entry is safer argued under clause (b) alone than loosely alongside clause (h). | Attack the timing precondition head-on for clauses (d), (e), and (f) — if the statement postdates the controversy, relevance fails outright regardless of reliability. |
| For clause (g), show the underlying transaction independently satisfies section 11(a), rather than treating clause (g) as a free-standing test. | For clause (c), test whether the statement was genuinely against the maker's interest at the time it was made, not merely inconvenient in hindsight. |

#### The limitation clock

None. These are relevance rules for statements offered at trial, not filing deadlines; the practical timing question that matters is internal to clauses (d), (e), and (f) themselves — whether the statement predates the dispute — not any external limitation period.

#### Interlocking matrix

| This provision | Interlocks with | Why |
|---|---|---|
| S. 26(g) | Section 11(a), covered in the first file in this chapter | Clause (g) expressly borrows section 11(a)'s own definition of a qualifying transaction rather than defining its own. |
| S. 26(b) | Chapter V's provisions on documentary and electronic-record proof | A business-course entry that is itself an electronic record still has to separately satisfy Chapter V's proof requirements once its relevance under clause (b) is established. |

#### The authorities

No confidently recalled leading authority is promoted for this categorical cluster specifically; practitioners relying on any one of clauses (b) through (h) should verify current commentary and recent decisions on that particular clause before argument, rather than assume the dying-declaration case law under clause (a) transfers across.

#### Strategy and drafting

In commercial and succession disputes, plead the specific lettered clause relied upon rather than citing "section 26" generally — a court testing a business ledger against clause (c)'s against-interest logic when clause (b)'s routine-business logic is what actually fits will apply the wrong trustworthiness test to the wrong fact. When resisting a relationship statement under clause (e) or (f), obtain and lead evidence of exactly when the statement was first made or recorded, since the timing precondition is usually the cleanest and most objectively provable ground of challenge available.

## Sections 27 to 30 — former testimony, business accounts, public records, and maps

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute A witness who cannot be recalled, and a ledger that cannot stand alone
Devika sues Farooq a second time over the same partnership accounting dispute, after the first suit was dismissed on a technical ground and refiled between the identical parties on the identical issues. Her key witness, Elena, testified fully and was cross-examined by Farooq's advocate in the first suit, but has since died. Devika wants to read Elena's former testimony into the second suit. Separately, Devika also produces her own account books showing an entry recording Farooq's debt to her, but has no other evidence beyond the entries themselves. Farooq's advocate argues that neither piece of proof should be allowed to do the work Devika wants it to do.
:::

::: story Two rescues, two very different limits
Section 27 rescues testimony that would otherwise be lost — a witness who has already been fully tested by cross-examination becomes unavailable a second time — but only within a tight proviso: the same parties or their representatives in interest, a genuine earlier opportunity to cross-examine, and substantially the same issues. Section 28 rescues a different problem — routine account-book entries that would otherwise each need a live witness to prove — but caps its own rescue with an explicit statutory qualifier: such entries are relevant, but can never, by themselves, be sufficient to fix anyone with liability. Sections 29 and 30 extend comparable relevance, without a comparable cap, to official records and to published maps or government plans, on the reasoning that the certainty of an official duty or a government-sanctioned publication process substitutes adequately for a live witness.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

> **Section 27 — Relevancy of certain evidence for proving, in subsequent proceeding, truth of facts therein stated.** Evidence given by a witness in a judicial proceeding, or before any person authorised by law to take it, is relevant for the purpose of proving, in a subsequent judicial proceeding, or in a later stage of the same judicial proceeding, the truth of the facts which it states, when the witness is dead or cannot be found, or is incapable of giving evidence, or is kept out of the way by the adverse party, or if his presence cannot be obtained without an amount of delay or expense which, under the circumstances of the case, the Court considers unreasonable:
>
> [[!Provided that the proceeding was between the same parties or their representatives in interest]]; that the adverse party in the first proceeding had the [[!right and opportunity to cross-examine]]; and the [[!questions in issue were substantially the same]] in the first as in the second proceeding.
>
> *Explanation.*—A criminal trial or inquiry shall be deemed to be a proceeding between the prosecutor and the accused within the meaning of this section.
>
> **Section 28 — Entries in books of account when relevant.** Entries in the books of account, including those maintained in an [[=electronic form]], regularly kept in the course of business are relevant whenever they refer to a matter into which the Court has to inquire, but such statements shall [[!not alone be sufficient evidence]] to charge any person with liability.
>
> **Section 29 — Relevancy of entry in public record or an electronic record made in performance of duty.** An entry in any public or other official book, register or record or an electronic record, stating a fact in issue or relevant fact, and made by a public servant in the discharge of his official duty, or by any other person in performance of a duty specially enjoined by the law of the country in which such book, register or record or an electronic record, is kept, is itself a relevant fact.
>
> **Section 30 — Relevancy of statements in maps, charts and plans.** Statements of facts in issue or relevant facts, made in published maps or charts generally offered for public sale, or in maps or plans made under the authority of the Central Government or any State Government, as to matters usually represented or stated in such maps, charts or plans, are themselves relevant facts.

*Illustration to section 28.* A sues B for one thousand rupees, and shows entries in his account books showing B to be indebted to him to this amount. The entries are relevant, but are not sufficient, without other evidence, to prove the debt.

| Statutory phrase | Deconstruction | Practical effect |
|---|---|---|
| "same parties or their representatives in interest... right and opportunity to cross-examine... substantially the same" | **Three conjunctive conditions for s. 27**, not alternatives | All three must hold together; failing any single one defeats the whole rescue, however unavailable the original witness now is. |
| "shall not alone be sufficient evidence" (s. 28) | **A built-in evidentiary cap**, not a mere relevance rule | Account-book entries get through the door but can never, by themselves, carry the case — some independent corroboration is always required. |
| "made by a public servant in the discharge of his official duty" (s. 29) | **No equivalent cap** | Unlike s. 28, section 29 carries no "not alone sufficient" qualifier — an official record entry can, in principle, stand on its own once relevant. |

::: proviso Section 27's three conditions are read together, not in isolation
A former proceeding that satisfies two of the three conditions but not the third does not partially qualify. If the questions in issue were only loosely similar, or if the adverse party never actually had a genuine chance to cross-examine (as opposed to a formal, unused opportunity), section 27 does not rescue the former testimony — the earlier evidence is excluded outright, not merely discounted for weight.
:::

### In plain English

**Section 27 is about giving a second life to testimony that already survived cross-examination once.** The reason the three conditions are so strict is that this section allows a court to rely on evidence the current adverse party never personally tested — the safeguard has to come from somewhere, and it comes from insisting the earlier cross-examination was a genuine, adequate substitute.

**Section 28's cap is the whole point of the section, not an afterthought.** A shopkeeper's ledger is exactly the kind of record most vulnerable to self-serving entries; the section lets it in because routine bookkeeping has some habitual reliability, but refuses to let a litigant win a case on their own unaudited books alone.

**Sections 29 and 30 trust official process instead of cross-examination.** Neither carries section 28's cap, because the reliability rationale is different — a public servant's official duty, or a government's map-publication process, is treated as a strong enough guarantee on its own, unlike a private party's business ledger.

## Sections 31 to 33 — public-nature facts in gazettes, foreign law, and how much of a statement must be proved

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute A Gazette notification, a foreign contract, and a sentence pulled out of context
In a suit about the status of a village road, one party proves it is a public way through a notification published in the Official Gazette. In an unrelated commercial dispute, the enforceability of a supply contract turns on a question of Singapore law, and neither party can produce a Singapore-qualified law expert in the time available before trial. And in a workplace dispute, an employer wants to lead only the single recorded sentence "I quit," without the rest of the same conversation in which the employee immediately said she was joking and would be at her desk the next morning.
:::

::: story Three provisions, three different problems with proving facts outside the courtroom
Section 31 lets a court accept, without calling a live witness, a fact of a public nature recited in a Central or State Act or in a government notification in the Official Gazette — the logic being that a formally published, government-sanctioned recital of a public fact does not need a witness to vouch for it. Section 32 extends comparable treatment to foreign law itself, letting a party prove what another country's law says through printed or electronic books purporting to contain that law, and through printed or electronic reports of that country's own court rulings, rather than requiring a foreign-law expert to travel and testify in person. Section 33 solves an entirely different kind of problem: once any part of a longer statement, conversation, or document is put into evidence, the section requires the court to be shown as much of the surrounding material as is necessary for a full and fair understanding of the statement's true nature and effect — a party cannot lead one sentence and leave the explaining or qualifying context behind.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

> **Section 31 — Relevancy of statement as to fact of public nature contained in certain Acts or notifications.** When the Court has to form an opinion as to the existence of any [[=fact of a public nature]], any statement of it, made in a recital contained in any Central Act or State Act or in a Central Government or State Government notification appearing in the respective Official Gazette or in any [[=printed paper or in electronic or digital form]] purporting to be such Gazette, is a relevant fact.
>
> **Section 32 — Relevancy of statements as to any law contained in law books including electronic or digital form.** When the Court has to form an opinion as to a law of any country, any statement of such law contained in a book purporting to be printed or published, including in electronic or digital form, under the authority of the Government of such country and to contain any such law, and any report of a ruling of the Courts of such country contained in a book, including in electronic or digital form, purporting to be a report of such rulings, is relevant.
>
> **Section 33 — How much of a statement is to be proved.** When any statement of which evidence is given forms part of a longer statement, or of a conversation or part of an isolated document, or is contained in a document which forms part of a book, or is contained in part of electronic record or of a connected series of letters or papers, evidence shall be given of [[!so much and no more of the statement]]... as the Court considers necessary in that particular case to the [[!full understanding of the nature and effect of the statement]], and of the circumstances under which it was made.

| Statutory phrase | Deconstruction | Practical effect |
|---|---|---|
| "notification appearing in the... Official Gazette or in any printed paper or in electronic or digital form purporting to be such Gazette" (s. 31) | **Publication format is irrelevant; official character is what matters** | A digital or online edition of the Gazette is treated exactly the same as a printed copy for this purpose. |
| "book purporting to be printed or published... under the authority of the Government of such country" (s. 32) | **A specific, government-sanctioned source requirement** | An informal summary, blog post, or unofficial commentary on foreign law does not qualify merely because it accurately states the law. |
| "so much and no more... as the Court considers necessary... to the full understanding" (s. 33) | **A completeness floor, not a completeness ceiling** | The rule requires enough context for fair understanding — it does not entitle either side to flood the record with the entire document regardless of relevance. |

::: proviso Section 33 cuts both ways
The completeness rule is not a one-way shield for whoever is resisting a quoted extract. It equally stops the resisting party from demanding the admission of irrelevant surrounding material that does nothing to aid understanding of the statement actually in evidence — the test is necessity for full and fair understanding, not an automatic right to introduce everything adjacent to the quoted words.
:::

### In plain English

**Sections 31 and 32 both trust an official publication process instead of a witness.** Neither requires the underlying fact to be independently corroborated — the formality of Gazette publication, or of a government-authorised foreign-law text, is treated as the reliability guarantee in itself.

**Section 32's source requirement is stricter than it looks.** "Purporting to be printed or published under the authority of the Government of such country" rules out convenient but unofficial sources — a well-written private treatise on foreign law is not automatically a substitute for an officially sanctioned text or ruling report.

**Section 33 exists so that quotation cannot become distortion.** The rule does not ban partial quotation outright; it insists that if a partial quotation would mislead the court about the statement's true nature without its context, that context must be supplied alongside it.

::::instinct The recorded sentence with no context

A private law firm's blog post, however accurate its summary of Singapore law might genuinely be, does not carry the government-sanctioned character the section demands. The party may well be stating the correct law, but has proved it through the wrong kind of source — the substance is right, the mode of proof is wrong, and section 32 does not bend to accommodate a more convenient source.

## Chapter II (part III) consolidation — is this statement getting into evidence, and on what basis?

Walking sections 26 to 33 in sequence answers a single practical question: a person cannot be brought to testify, or a piece of testimony or a record already exists outside the witness box — can it come in anyway, and if so, on what specific footing?

1. **Is the maker of the statement dead, untraceable, incapacitated, or producible only at unreasonable cost?** If so, move through section 26's own list: is it about the cause of the maker's death (clause (a)); a routine business entry (clause (b)); against the maker's own interest (clause (c)); a pre-dispute opinion on a public matter (clause (d)); a pre-dispute relationship statement (clauses (e)-(f)); a transaction document under section 11(a) (clause (g)); or a collective expression of feeling (clause (h))?
2. **Is it not a statement by an unavailable person at all, but testimony already given in an earlier proceeding?** Test it against section 27's three conjunctive conditions — same parties, genuine cross-examination opportunity, substantially the same issues — before relying on it in place of a live witness.
3. **Is it a business record, an official record, or a published map or plan?** Section 28 lets an account-book entry in, but never lets it stand alone; sections 29 and 30 extend relevance to official and government-sanctioned records without that same cap.
4. **Is it a recital of a public-nature fact in a Gazette, or a foreign-law text or ruling report?** Sections 31 and 32 accept these on the strength of their official, government-sanctioned publication, provided the source genuinely carries that character.
5. **Finally, however the statement gets in, is only part of a longer statement, conversation, or document being offered?** Section 33 requires enough of the remainder to be shown alongside it for the Court to fully and fairly understand what was actually said and in what circumstances — the last checkpoint that applies regardless of which earlier gate the statement passed through.
