# Chapter V (part IV) — Of Documentary Evidence: public documents (ss. 74-77)

Chapter V, "Of Documentary Evidence," runs ss. 56-93. This fourth file covers the Act's own "Public documents" sub-heading: what makes a document "public" rather than "private" (s. 74), the custodian's duty to supply a certified copy (s. 75), the effect of that certified copy as proof of contents (s. 76), and the further, more specific routes by which particular kinds of public documents — government notifications, legislative proceedings, proclamations, foreign-country records — may be proved (s. 77).

## Sections 74 to 77 — public documents, certified copies, and how public documents are proved

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute
A party wants to prove what a government notification said. Calling the officer who issued it, or producing the original file from a government department, would be slow and often impossible — government records are not handed out to be carried into a courtroom. Sections 74 to 77 solve this by recognising a category of "public documents" whose institutional pedigree lets a certified copy, or in some cases a Gazette extract or a specified kind of record, stand in for the original — without needing the primary-evidence-first machinery Chapter V otherwise insists on.
:::

::: story
Nobody expects a government department to send its original land records or its legislative journals to sit in a courtroom exhibit box. The law's answer is not to make such documents harder to prove — it is to trust the institution's own certification process instead of insisting on the physical original every time.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

> **74.**(1) The following documents are [[=public documents]]:— (a) documents forming the acts, or records of the acts— (i) of the sovereign authority; (ii) of official bodies and tribunals; and (iii) of public officers, legislative, judicial and executive of India or of a foreign country; (b) public records kept in any State or Union territory of private documents.
>
> (2) All other documents except the documents referred to in sub-section (1) are private.
>
> **75.** Every public officer having the custody of a public document, which any person has a right to inspect, [[!shall give]] that person on demand a copy of it on payment of the legal fees therefor, together with a certificate written at the foot of such copy that it is a true copy of such document or part thereof, as the case may be, and such certificate shall be dated and subscribed by such officer with his name and his official title, and shall be sealed, whenever such officer is authorised by law to make use of a seal; and such copies so certified shall be called [[=certified copies]].
>
> Explanation.—Any officer who, by the ordinary course of official duty, is authorised to deliver such copies, shall be deemed to have the custody of such documents within the meaning of this section.
>
> **76.** Such certified copies [[?may be produced]] in proof of the contents of the public documents or parts of the public documents of which they purport to be copies.
>
> **77.** The following public documents [[?may be proved as follows]]:— (a) Acts, orders or notifications of the Central Government in any of its Ministries and Departments or of any State Government or any Department of any State Government or Union territory Administration— (i) by the records of the Departments, certified by the head of those Departments respectively; or (ii) by any document purporting to be printed by order of any such Government; (b) the proceedings of Parliament or a State Legislature, by the journals of those bodies respectively, or by published Acts or abstracts, or by copies purporting to be printed by order of the Government concerned; (c) proclamations, orders or Regulations issued by the President of India or the Governor of a State or the Administrator or Lieutenant Governor of a Union territory, by copies or extracts contained in the Official Gazette; (d) the Acts of the Executive or the proceedings of the Legislature of a foreign country, by journals published by their authority, or commonly received in that country as such, or by a copy certified under the seal of the country or sovereign, or by a recognition thereof in any Central Act; (e) the proceedings of a municipal or local body in a State, by a copy of such proceedings, certified by the legal keeper thereof, or by a printed book purporting to be published by the authority of such body; (f) public documents of any other class in a foreign country, by the original or by a copy certified by the legal keeper thereof, with a certificate under the seal of a Notary Public, or of an Indian Consul or diplomatic agent, that the copy is duly certified by the officer having the legal custody of the original, and upon proof of the character of the document according to the law of the foreign country.

| Text | What it does | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| S. 74(1), clauses (a)-(b) | Defines "public document" as a closed list: acts/records of sovereign, official, or judicial-legislative-executive authority, plus public records of private documents | A document's institutional origin, not its subject matter, decides whether it is public — a privately drafted document can still be a "public document" once it is kept as a public record under clause (b) |
| S. 74(2) | Makes every other document private by residual definition | Leaves no third category — classification is binary, not a matter of degree |
| S. 75 and its Explanation | Makes it the custodian's duty to supply a properly dated, subscribed, and (where applicable) sealed certified copy on demand and payment of fees | Fixes exactly what a "certified copy" must contain to count as one — an uncertified or incompletely certified copy is not a certified copy at all |
| S. 76 | Lets a certified copy prove the contents of the public document it purports to copy | This is the payoff for classifying a document as public: the certified copy displaces the need for the original |
| S. 77, clauses (a)-(f) | Supplies further, more specific proof routes for six named categories of public document — government notifications, legislative proceedings, presidential/gubernatorial proclamations, foreign executive/legislative records, municipal proceedings, and other foreign public documents | Recognises that the general certified-copy route of ss. 75-76 is not always the most practical or available route for every kind of public document, and supplies tailored alternatives |

::: proviso A private document does not stop being provable as one just because a copy of it sits in a public office
Section 74(1)(b) makes "public records kept... of private documents" a public document in its own right. This does not convert the underlying private document itself into a public document for every purpose — it means the public record (for instance, a registration copy held by a Registrar) can itself be proved through the certified-copy route, even though the document it records originated privately. The private original and the public record of it are treated separately.
:::

### In plain English

**A document earns the "public" label by where it comes from, not by how important it is.** Once a document qualifies under section 74(1) — because a sovereign, official, or judicial/legislative/executive authority made or recorded it, or because it is a public record kept of a private document — the law no longer insists on the original or a live witness to its making. A properly certified copy, obtained from the custodian under section 75, can prove the contents instead, under section 76.

Section 77 goes further for six specific kinds of public document, recognising that a plain certified copy is not always the most natural way to prove a Gazette notification, a parliamentary proceeding, or a foreign country's records — each gets its own tailored route. None of this displaces section 74(2)'s residual rule: anything not caught by section 74(1) stays a private document, proved the ordinary way.

### Test your instinct

::::instinct Scenario 1 — the private will lodged with a public registry
A testator's will, an entirely private document when drafted, is later registered and kept in the Sub-Registrar's public records. A party wants to prove its contents through a certified copy from the Registrar rather than producing the original or calling witnesses to its execution. Can she do this?
:::ruling
Yes, for the record itself. Section 74(1)(b) makes "public records kept... of private documents" a public document in its own right — the Registrar's public record of the will qualifies, and a certified copy of that record can be obtained under section 75 and used under section 76. This does not mean the will's execution is proved merely by producing the certified copy of the registered record; if execution itself is disputed, the ordinary execution-proof rules in sections 65 to 71 still apply to the underlying document. The certified copy proves what the public record contains, not that the testator validly executed the will.
:::
::::

::::instinct Scenario 2 — the incomplete certificate
A party obtains a copy of a public document from the relevant officer, but the copy carries no date, no official title, and no seal, even though the officer is authorised to use one. The party tenders it as a certified copy under section 76. Is it admissible on that basis?
:::ruling
No. Section 75 specifies precisely what a certified copy must contain: a certificate that it is a true copy, dated, subscribed with the officer's name and official title, and sealed wherever the officer is authorised to use a seal. A copy missing these elements is not a "certified copy" within the meaning the Act gives that term, however genuine the underlying document might be — and section 76's shortcut is available only to a copy that actually meets section 75's requirements. The party must go back and obtain a properly certified copy.
:::
::::

::::instinct Scenario 3 — proving a foreign government's notification
A party needs to prove the contents of a foreign government's official notification. She tenders an ordinary photocopy obtained informally, with no notarial or consular certification and no proof of the document's character under the foreign country's own law. Is this sufficient under section 77?
:::ruling
No. Clause (f) of section 77 governs public documents of this kind — "any other class in a foreign country" — and requires either the original, or a copy certified by the legal keeper of the original together with a certificate under the seal of a Notary Public or an Indian Consul or diplomatic agent that the copy is duly certified, and proof of the document's character according to the foreign country's own law. An informal, uncertified photocopy satisfies none of this. The general certified-copy route under sections 75-76 is not a substitute here either, since it presupposes an Indian public officer with custody duties under section 75 — the party must instead follow clause (f)'s specific, more demanding route.
:::
::::

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio
No single authority is promoted here. The public/private document classification and the certified-copy mechanism are worked out through the Act's own text.

#### Sword and shield
| | As a sword (offering evidence) | As a shield (resisting evidence) |
|---|---|---|
| S. 74 | Identify the specific section 74(1) clause a document falls under before relying on any certified-copy shortcut | Challenge the classification itself — a document is not "public" merely because a government office happens to hold a copy of it |
| S. 75 | Request a certified copy formally, in writing, and check on receipt that it is dated, subscribed with name and official title, and sealed where required | Inspect any certified copy tendered against you for exactly these formal requirements before conceding it is properly certified |
| S. 76 | Rely on a properly certified copy instead of chasing the original through a government department | Object if what is tendered as a "certified copy" does not actually meet section 75's certificate requirements |
| S. 77 | Match the specific document to its named clause — Gazette extracts for proclamations, departmental records or Government Printing Press documents for notifications, the appropriate foreign-document route for foreign records | Test whether the tendering party has actually followed the specific route section 77 prescribes for that category, rather than a generic certified copy |

#### The limitation clock
None of its own. As elsewhere in this chapter, the operative discipline is to object to a defectively certified copy, or to a document's public/private classification, when it is tendered — not for the first time on appeal.

#### The authorities
No confidently recalled leading authority is promoted for this cluster.

#### Strategy and drafting
Always request certified copies formally and in writing, and inspect them on receipt against section 75's checklist — date, subscription with name and official title, and seal where the officer is authorised to use one — before relying on them at trial, since a defect caught early can be fixed by requesting a fresh copy, while one caught for the first time in the witness box cannot. Where a document could be proved either through the general certified-copy route or a specific section 77 clause, prefer the specific route where it exists — it is usually harder to challenge than a generic certified copy. When resisting a public document, do not concede its classification under section 74 automatically; test whether it genuinely falls within a named clause before accepting that the more relaxed proof regime applies at all.

## Chapter V (part IV) consolidation — is this a public document, and has it been proved the right way?

1. **Does the document fall within section 74(1)'s closed list — an act or record of sovereign, official, or judicial/legislative/executive authority, or a public record kept of a private document?** If not, it is private under section 74(2), and this cluster's shortcuts do not apply.
2. **If public, is a certified copy being relied on, and does it actually meet section 75's certificate requirements — dated, subscribed with name and official title, sealed where required?** An incomplete certificate is not a certified copy at all.
3. **Does the specific document fall within one of section 77's six named categories, with its own tailored proof route?** If so, that route, not the general certified-copy route, is the one to use.
4. **Where the underlying document is itself private but held as a public record, has the distinction between proving the public record and proving the private document's own execution been kept separate?**
