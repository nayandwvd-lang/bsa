# Chapter V — Of Documentary Evidence

Chapter V (ss. 56–93) covers primary and secondary evidence, proof of documents, and — the cluster below — the admissibility of electronic and digital records. Only that cluster is written so far; the remainder of the chapter (proof of execution, public documents, presumptions as to documents) is future work.

## Sections 61 to 63 — Admissibility of electronic records

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute A WhatsApp screenshot is not evidence just because it is real.

Anita's phone has a WhatsApp chat that settles her case outright — on 3 January, Bharat typed, in so many words, that he owed her ₹8 lakh. She takes a screenshot, prints it on office letterhead, and hands the printout to her advocate. In court, before a single word of the chat is read out, Bharat's counsel objects — not that the chat is fake, but that Anita cannot even show it to the judge yet.

He is right, and the reason is not a technicality. It is the entire point of ss. 61 to 63 of the Adhiniyam. A screenshot, a printout, a CD, a cloud backup — none of these is an "original" the way a signed letter is. Each is a copy manufactured by a machine, and a machine can be made to produce a false copy as easily as a true one, and far more invisibly than a forged signature. So the Adhiniyam does not let the court trust a printout because it looks genuine. It requires the party tendering it to first prove, through a certificate about the machine that produced it, that the machine was working properly and was fed information in the ordinary course of business — before the court so much as reads the chat.

Miss that certificate and the chat log is not weak evidence. It is not evidence at all. That single distinction — between a document that is true and a document that is admissible — is the most litigated question in this cluster, and it is why advocates who are excellent with a paper file routinely lose at the very first hurdle with a phone.
:::

::: story "The record is authentic" and "the record is admissible" are two different sentences, and ss. 61 to 63 answer only the second.

Three questions get asked about any piece of digital evidence, and this cluster answers only one of them cleanly.

The first question is whether the record is genuine — was the chat really sent by Bharat, or edited afterwards; is the CCTV clip continuous, or spliced. That is a question of fact, decided at the end of trial on the whole of the evidence, and ss. 61 to 63 have almost nothing to say about it.

The second question — the one this cluster exists to answer — is whether the court may look at the copy at all before that factual enquiry even begins. Before the Adhiniyam, courts sometimes refused to look at electronic material on the bare ground that it was electronic, treating a printout as inherently less trustworthy than paper. Section 61 closes that door outright: an electronic or digital record cannot be kept out merely because it is electronic. But s. 61 buys that equal treatment at a price, stated in its own text — "subject to section 63". The record earns admission through the section 63 gateway, not by default.

The third question, which practitioners often collapse into the second, is whether the certificate itself is truthful. It usually is not contested — the certifying witness is rarely cross-examined on whether the computer really was working properly — which is exactly why the certificate requirement is not paperwork. It is the one safeguard in the whole scheme that actually gets tested, because it is the one thing a party can be made to produce or fail to produce, in a way that "was the chat edited" cannot be resolved except by a forensic fight most litigants cannot afford. *Anvar P.V.* and *Arjun Panditrao Khotkar*, discussed below, are fights about the second question, not the first — and reading them as though they decide authenticity is the commonest misreading in this area of law.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

Three sections, read together, do the whole job. Section 61 declares that electronic records are not second-class documents. Section 62 is a one-line bridge, sending every question about *how* to prove the contents of an electronic record to s. 63. Section 63 is the actual gateway — a single long, "notwithstanding"-led provision built on four cumulative conditions in sub-section (2) and a certificate requirement in sub-section (4) that most of the reported case law is actually about. Watch for the double machinery in sub-section (4): the certificate is not just described there, it is required to be in the form set out in the Schedule to the Adhiniyam, filled in two parts by two different people — a change from the position under the old Act that the drafting notes below will come back to.

> **61. Electronic or digital record.** — Nothing in this Adhiniyam [[!shall]] apply to deny the admissibility of an [[=electronic or digital record]] in the evidence on the ground that it is an electronic or digital record and such record shall, subject to section 63, have [[!the same legal effect, validity and enforceability]] as other document.
>
> **62. Special provisions as to evidence relating to electronic record.** — The contents of electronic records [[?may]] be proved in accordance with the provisions of section 63.
>
> **63. Admissibility of electronic records.** — **(1)** Notwithstanding anything contained in this Adhiniyam, any information contained in an electronic record which is printed on paper, stored, recorded or copied in optical or magnetic media or semiconductor memory which is produced by a computer or any communication device or otherwise stored, recorded or copied in any electronic form (hereinafter referred to as the [[=computer output]]) [[!shall be deemed to be also a document]], if the conditions mentioned in this section [[!are satisfied]] in relation to the information and computer in question and [[!shall be admissible]] in any proceedings, without further proof or production of the original, as evidence of any contents of the original or of any fact stated therein of which direct evidence would be admissible.
>
> **(2)** The conditions referred to in sub-section (1) in respect of a computer output [[!shall]] be [[=the following]], namely:— (a) the computer output containing the information was produced by the computer or communication device during the period over which it was regularly used to create, store or process information for the purposes of any activity regularly carried on over that period by the person having lawful control over its use; (b) during the said period, information of the kind contained in the electronic record was regularly fed into the computer or communication device in the ordinary course of the said activities; (c) throughout the material part of the said period, the computer or communication device was operating properly, or, if not, was not such as to affect the electronic record or the accuracy of its contents; and (d) the information contained in the electronic record reproduces or is derived from such information fed into the computer or communication device in the ordinary course of the said activities.
>
> **(3)** Where over any period the function of creating, storing or processing information for the purposes of an activity regularly carried on over that period was regularly performed by means of one or more computers or communication devices, whether in standalone mode, on a computer system, on a computer network, on a computer resource enabling information creation or providing information processing and storage, or through an intermediary, all the computers or communication devices used for that purpose during that period [[!shall be treated]] for the purposes of this section as constituting a single computer or communication device.
>
> **(4)** In any proceeding where it is desired to give a statement in evidence by virtue of this section, a certificate doing any of the following things [[!shall be submitted]] along with the electronic record **at each instance where it is being submitted for admission**, namely:— (a) identifying the electronic record containing the statement and describing the manner in which it was produced; (b) giving such particulars of any device involved in the production of that electronic record as may be appropriate for the purpose of showing that the electronic record was produced by a computer or a communication device referred to in clauses (a) to (e) of sub-section (3); (c) dealing with any of the matters to which the conditions mentioned in sub-section (2) relate, and purporting to be signed by a person in charge of the computer or communication device or the management of the relevant activities (whichever is appropriate) **and an expert** shall be evidence of any matter stated in the certificate, and for that purpose it [[!shall be sufficient]] for a matter to be stated to the best of the knowledge and belief of the person stating it, in the certificate specified in the Schedule.
>
> **(5)** For the purposes of this section, information [[!shall be taken]] to be supplied to a computer or communication device whether supplied directly or, with or without human intervention, by means of any appropriate equipment; and a computer output [[!shall be taken]] to have been produced by a computer or communication device whether produced directly or by other electronic means referred to in clauses (a) to (e) of sub-section (3).

**Statutory mechanics.**

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| "*Nothing in this Adhiniyam shall apply to deny*" (s. 61) | **Mandatory, protective** | Forecloses the argument that an electronic record is inadmissible merely for being electronic. It is not, on its own, a route to admission — it removes an objection, it does not answer the gateway question. |
| "**may**" (s. 62) | **Discretionary in form, not in substance** | Reads as an option, but in practice there is no other route: the contents of an electronic record can only be proved "in accordance with" s. 63, so a party who skips s. 63 has not proved the contents at all. |
| "**shall be deemed to be also a document**" (s. 63(1)) | **Mandatory** | Once the s. 63(2) conditions are met, the computer output IS a document for evidentiary purposes — no separate discretion to exclude it for being a copy. |
| The four conditions in s. 63(2) | **Mandatory, cumulative** | All four must hold. Failure on any single one defeats admissibility under this section, independent of the certificate. |
| "**shall be submitted** … **at each instance**" (s. 63(4)) | **Mandatory, per instance** | Not a one-time filing. A certificate that satisfied an earlier hearing does not travel automatically to a later tender of the same record. |
| "*Notwithstanding anything contained in this Adhiniyam*" (s. 63(1)) | **Overriding clause** | Section 63 is a self-contained code for computer output; the ordinary primary/secondary evidence rules elsewhere in this Chapter do not supply a back door around it. |

::: proviso The certificate is not paperwork — it is the admissibility gate itself

Every one of the arguments below actually turns on this one sentence in s. 63(4): a certificate "shall be submitted along with the electronic record **at each instance** where it is being submitted for admission." Two consequences follow, and both are where cases are actually won and lost.

First, the certificate is a condition precedent to admissibility, not a matter going to weight. A court cannot admit the computer output and then decide later how much to trust it if no certificate was ever filed — the record never became a "document" under sub-section (1) in the first place.

Second, "at each instance" means the certificate is tied to the act of tendering, not to the record. A certificate filed in one proceeding, or at an earlier hearing of the same proceeding, does not automatically validate a later, separate tender of the same electronic record. *Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal*, (2020) 7 SCC 1, held the equivalent requirement under the old s. 65B(4) mandatory, subject only to a narrow exception for a party who has done everything within its power to obtain the certificate and the person in control of the device has declined to cooperate — in which case the party may seek a direction from the court, rather than being left remediless. Most cases do not die at that narrow exception. They die because nobody asked for the certificate at all.
:::

::: proviso The Schedule certificate now has two signatories, not one

Section 63(4) requires the certificate to be "signed by a person in charge of the computer or communication device or the management of the relevant activities … **and an expert**", in the form set out in the Schedule to the Adhiniyam. The Schedule is printed in two parts: Part A, headed "To be filled by the Party", and Part B, headed "To be filled by the Expert." Part A asks the device custodian to identify the device (make, model, serial number, IMEI/UID/MAC as applicable), describe how it was used, and state a hash value for the record, obtained through a named algorithm (SHA1, SHA256, MD5, or another "legally acceptable standard"), with a hash report to be enclosed.

This is a real gate, not a drafting flourish. A certificate signed only by the device custodian, in the old single-signatory style that satisfied s. 65B(4), does not on its face satisfy s. 63(4) — the text contemplates both signatures, and the Schedule builds an entire second part around the expert's role. Whether a court will insist on the expert signature in every case — a personal WhatsApp screenshot has no natural "expert" custodian the way a bank's server log does — is not yet settled by reported Supreme Court authority, because the Adhiniyam is too recent for that question to have reached it. Treat the two-signatory, hash-value certificate as the default expectation until a court says otherwise, not as an optional upgrade.
:::

### In plain English

**Section 61 answers a question courts used to ask and should not ask again.** Before the Adhiniyam, a party sometimes had to first persuade a sceptical court that an electronic record deserved to be treated as a document at all. Section 61 removes that argument by name: an electronic or digital record cannot be denied admissibility on the ground that it is electronic. It buys nothing by itself — the words "subject to section 63" in the same sentence mean the record still has to walk through the s. 63 door — but it forecloses the argument that the door should not exist.

**Section 62 is a bridge, not a rule of its own.** It looks discretionary — "may be proved" — but there is no alternative route it is choosing between. Read alone, it would be a dead end; read with s. 63, it is the sentence that tells you where to go next.

**Section 63(1) does the actual work of deeming.** A computer output — anything printed, stored, recorded, or copied from a computer or communication device, in any electronic form — becomes "deemed to be also a document" once the s. 63(2) conditions are satisfied. Once deemed a document, no further proof or production of the original device is required; the printout stands in for the original.

**The four conditions in sub-section (2), each with a bolded lead.** *Regular use* — the device was being used regularly, for the relevant activity, over the period in question; a one-off borrowed laptop used to fabricate a single document the day before trial fails this condition on its face. *Regular feeding* — information of the relevant kind was fed into the device regularly, in the ordinary course of that activity, not specially created for the litigation. *Proper operation* — the device worked correctly throughout the material period, or, if it malfunctioned, the malfunction did not affect the accuracy of the record; a known-faulty server does not automatically defeat the record, but the malfunction must be shown not to have touched the specific content relied on. *Accurate reproduction* — the electronic record actually reproduces, or is derived from, the information that was fed in — not a manually retyped summary presented as the original output.

**Sub-section (3) answers the "which computer" problem.** Modern systems rarely run on one machine. Where the relevant function was performed across multiple devices — standalone, on a network, on a cloud computer resource, or through an intermediary — sub-section (3) treats the whole set as a single computer or communication device for the purposes of s. 63, so a party is not defeated merely because a bank's transaction record passed through several servers before reaching the printout.

**Sub-section (4) has two moving parts practitioners routinely separate wrongly.** The certificate must (a) identify the record and describe how it was produced, (b) give particulars of the device sufficient to show it falls within sub-section (3), and (c) address the sub-section (2) conditions — and it must be signed by the device custodian **and** an expert, in the Schedule form, and filed at every instance the record is tendered. Treating this as one signature by one convenient witness is the single most common way this gateway is missed.

**Sub-section (5) closes two definitional gaps.** Information counts as "supplied" to a device whether a human typed it in directly or it arrived by automated means; a computer output counts as "produced by" the device whether it came out directly or through further automated processing. Both clauses exist so that a party cannot argue a record falls outside s. 63 merely because a human did not personally key in every entry, or because the printout passed through an extra piece of software before appearing on paper.

### What changed from the Indian Evidence Act

> **Indian Evidence Act, 1872, s. 65B → BSA 2023, ss. 61–63.** One old section has become three new ones. Confirmed against `reference/bare-act/bsa-2023-full-text.txt`: BSA s. 63 carries the operative certificate machinery that was all of s. 65B; ss. 61 and 62 are new or restructured framing provisions that did not exist as separate sections under the 1872 Act.

**Delta bucket, stated section by section, because the three sections do not all fall in the same bucket.**

- **Section 61 — new provision.** The Evidence Act, 1872 had no equivalent express declaration that an electronic record cannot be denied admissibility for being electronic. Courts derived that position, where they reached it at all, from case law and from s. 65B's general scheme. Section 61 makes it a standalone rule. There is no direct IEA ancestor; case law construing s. 65B is analogy here, not direct authority.
- **Section 62 — renumbered with drafting changes.** It performs the bridging role that the opening words of s. 65B(1) performed, now pulled out into its own section. The substance is unchanged; the case law on s. 65B applies to the bridge it describes.
- **Section 63 — substantively amended.** The core structure of s. 65B(1)–(5) survives, but with real changes: "computer" becomes "computer or communication device" throughout, extending the section unambiguously to mobile phones and similar devices rather than leaving that to interpretation; "semiconductor memory" is added to the list of storage media in sub-section (1); and, most significantly, sub-section (4) now points to a prescribed Schedule certificate in two parts — one for the device custodian, one for an expert, including a mandatory hash value — where s. 65B(4) required only a single certificate from a person occupying a responsible official position, in no prescribed form.

**Side by side, where the text actually changed.**

| IEA 1872, s. 65B | BSA 2023, s. 63 |
|---|---|
| "…produced by a **computer** (hereinafter referred to as the computer output)…" | "…produced by a **computer or any communication device** or otherwise stored, recorded or copied in any electronic form (hereinafter referred to as the computer output)…" |
| Conditions in sub-s. (2) refer throughout to "the computer" | Conditions in sub-s. (2) refer throughout to "the computer **or communication device**" |
| Sub-s. (4) certificate: "signed by a person occupying a responsible official position in relation to the operation of the relevant device or the management of the relevant activities" — **no prescribed form** | Sub-s. (4) certificate: signed by "a person in charge of the computer or communication device or the management of the relevant activities … **and an expert**", **in the form specified in the Schedule** (Parts A and B, including a hash value) |

**What survives, and what does not, from the old case law.** The *Navjot Sandhu → Anvar P.V. → Arjun Panditrao* line was decided entirely under s. 65B. The core holding of *Anvar P.V.* and *Arjun Panditrao* — that the certificate is a mandatory condition precedent to admissibility of secondary electronic evidence, not a mere procedural formality — turns on language ("shall be deemed to be also a document", the certificate requirement in sub-section (4)) that BSA s. 63 reproduces in substance. That reasoning should carry over to s. 63 on the same logic. What does **not** automatically carry over is anything a judgment said about the *form* of the certificate or who could sign it, because the Schedule's two-signatory, hash-value requirement did not exist for those courts to consider. A certificate that satisfied *Arjun Panditrao*'s standard for s. 65B(4) — one competent signatory, addressing the right topics — will not on its face satisfy s. 63(4), which textually asks for two.

**What a practitioner must now do differently.** Do not file the old single-signatory s. 65B(4)-style certificate and assume it survives the transition. Obtain the Schedule form, complete Part A through the device custodian and Part B through a person qualified to speak as an expert, compute and record a hash value using a named algorithm, and file the certificate afresh at every hearing at which the record is tendered — not once, and not by analogy to the pre-2024 practice.

### Test your instinct

::::instinct The chat log with no certificate at all

On 3 January, Bharat typed to Anita, "I'll pay the ₹8 lakh by March, don't worry." Anita's advocate, Farhan, printed the screenshot from her phone and tendered it at trial with nothing else — no certificate, no witness from a phone company, nothing about the device.

Judge Gokhale is handed the printout. Bharat's counsel objects the moment it is offered, before either side argues about whether the words are really Bharat's.

Should the objection succeed?

:::ruling Reveal the ruling
Yes. The printout is a computer output within s. 63(1), and no s. 63(4) certificate has been filed at all — not a defective one, simply none. The four sub-section (2) conditions are never even reached, because the certificate is the mechanism by which a party puts them before the court in the first place. The chat is not weighed and found unconvincing; it is never admitted. Anita's remedy is to go back and obtain the certificate — from herself, as the device's custodian, addressing the four conditions — and tender the record again, with the certificate, at the next opportunity.
::::

::::instinct The laptop itself, produced in court

Chetan, a forensic examiner, brings Bharat's own laptop to the witness box, powers it on in front of Judge Gokhale, and shows the WhatsApp Web session with the same chat still logged in. He deposes that the laptop is Bharat's, that he has not altered it, and that the chat is stored on it exactly as received.

Bharat's counsel objects that no s. 63(4) certificate has been filed for the laptop.

Does the objection succeed?

:::ruling Reveal the ruling
No. Section 63 governs computer *output* — a copy, printout, or other secondary reproduction of what is on a device. Where the device itself is produced and its custodian deposes to it directly, that is primary evidence of the record stored on it, proved the ordinary way, through a witness who can be cross-examined about the device itself. There is no computer output being tendered, so there is nothing for s. 63(4) to certify. This is the distinction *Arjun Panditrao Khotkar* drew explicitly under the old s. 65B, and it survives unchanged onto s. 63: producing the original device is always an available route around the certificate, precisely because it is not the route s. 63 was written to police.
::::

::::instinct One certificate, two hearings

Anita filed a proper, correctly signed s. 63(4) certificate for the WhatsApp chat at the first hearing before the trial court, and it was accepted without objection. The suit is later remanded for a fresh trial on an unrelated point. At the fresh trial, Anita's advocate tenders the same printout again, relying on the certificate already on the earlier record, without filing anything new.

Does the earlier certificate carry over?

:::ruling Reveal the ruling
No. Section 63(4) requires the certificate "at each instance where it is being submitted for admission" — the tender at the fresh trial is a new instance, not a continuation of the old one. The certificate must be filed again, even though nothing about the device or the record has changed and even though no one disputes the earlier certificate was valid when it was filed. This is the "per instance, not per record" reading of the proviso above, and it is exactly the kind of avoidable, purely mechanical failure that costs a party an otherwise clean case.
::::

::::instinct The bank official who has left, and will not sign

A cheque-bounce complaint turns on a bank's CCTV footage showing Bharat depositing a forged instrument. Deepak, Anita's advocate in the complaint, needs a s. 63(4) certificate from the bank official who managed the branch's server at the relevant time. That official has since resigned, moved abroad, and does not respond to any request. The bank's current manager refuses to certify a system he did not personally operate at the relevant time.

Deepak has written twice, waited six weeks, and even asked the bank's head office in writing. Nobody will sign.

Is the footage simply lost as evidence?

:::ruling Reveal the ruling
Not necessarily. *Arjun Panditrao Khotkar* carved out a narrow exception, construing the equivalent s. 65B(4) requirement: where a party has done everything within its power to obtain the certificate and the person in control of the relevant device declines to cooperate, the party is not left remediless — it may apply to the court for a direction, rather than treating the certificate as simply unobtainable and abandoning the evidence. Deepak's documented, repeated requests are exactly the kind of record that supports invoking that exception; a party who never asked would get no such indulgence. This is a narrow escape route, not a routine one — most parties who fail the certificate requirement failed to ask at all, not because they were genuinely refused.
::::

::::instinct The genuine confession nobody certified

In a domestic-violence proceeding, Deepa recorded Bharat, on her own phone, admitting on tape that he had struck her the previous week. The recording is unedited, unremarkable technically, and — everyone privately agrees, including Bharat's own counsel off the record — genuine. Deepa never obtained any certificate for it; there was no separate "device custodian" to ask, because she made the recording herself on her own phone and kept it there.

At trial, her advocate tenders the recording with only Deepa's own testimony that she made it and has not altered it — no s. 63(4) certificate, on the view that she herself is the obvious and only custodian and her testimony should be enough.

Is the recording admissible?

:::ruling Reveal the ruling
No — and Deepa loses this point even though the recording is genuine and even though everyone in the room believes it. Section 63(4) does not excuse the certificate merely because the custodian and the witness are the same person; if anything, that makes it easier to comply, not harder, because Deepa herself can complete Part A of the Schedule certificate as the device's custodian. What she cannot do is skip the certificate and substitute her own oral assurance for it, because the whole scheme of s. 63 treats the certificate, not a witness's say-so, as the gateway. This is precisely the case the schema's own honesty rule exists for: the fact is true, the recording is real, and the technically correct outcome is still exclusion, because the point of a certificate requirement is to not depend on whether the court finds a witness credible. A witness who is believed is not a substitute for a certificate that was never filed.
::::

::: warning Drafting trap
Do not plead genuineness of an electronic record as though it were a substitute for the s. 63(4) certificate. "The recording is authentic and unaltered" is an argument about weight; it answers nothing about admissibility, and a court that has not yet admitted the record has no occasion to weigh it at all.
:::

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

**Arjun Panditrao Khotkar v. Kailash Kushanrao Gorantyal**, (2020) 7 SCC 1 — three-judge Bench.

*Facts, in one line.* An election petition turned on speeches proved through secondary electronic recordings tendered without the s. 65B(4) certificate having been properly proved at trial.

**Ratio.** The certificate required by s. 65B(4) of the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (BSA s. 63(4)'s direct predecessor) is a mandatory condition precedent to the admissibility of secondary evidence of an electronic record — it cannot be dispensed with "in the interest of justice" — subject only to a narrow exception where the applicant has done everything within its power to obtain the certificate and the person in control of the relevant device or system has declined to cooperate, in which case the applicant may seek a direction from the court rather than being left remediless. The Court also confirmed that where the original device itself is produced and its owner or custodian gives direct evidence about it, that is primary evidence and s. 65B does not apply at all.

**Practitioner takeaway.** "The certificate under section 63(4) is a condition precedent to admissibility, not a matter of weight, and this Court has no discretion to admit secondary electronic evidence in its absence."

**Its limits.** *Arjun Panditrao* says nothing about the Schedule's two-signatory, hash-value certificate format introduced by BSA s. 63(4) — that machinery did not exist under the old Act. Do not assume a certificate that satisfies *Arjun Panditrao*'s standard for content automatically satisfies the newer form requirement; check the Schedule itself. The judgment also does not relax the "at each instance" requirement — its non-cooperation exception is about *obtaining* a certificate, not about *reusing* one that was validly obtained for an earlier tender.

**BSA status.** IEA-era judgment, construing the direct predecessor of BSA s. 63(4). The ratio on mandatoriness and on the primary-evidence escape route should survive onto s. 63 because the operative language is reproduced in substance; the ratio's application to certificate *form* does not survive unchanged, because the form itself has changed.

#### Sword and shield

| Used offensively (tendering the record) | Used defensively (objecting to it) |
|---|---|
| File the Schedule certificate, both parts, with a computed hash value, at the outset — do not wait to be challenged. | Object the moment the record is offered, before the content is read into evidence — an admissibility objection raised after the fact is far weaker. |
| If the natural custodian will not cooperate, document every request in writing before applying to the court under the *Arjun Panditrao* exception. | Check whether the certificate was filed for *this* tender, at *this* hearing — a certificate from an earlier stage of the same proceeding is not automatically good for a later one. |
| Where the original device can be produced, consider that route instead of the certificate route entirely — it avoids s. 63 altogether. | Test whether the certificate purports to be signed by both the device custodian and an expert, per the Schedule — a single-signatory certificate is a live target. |

#### The limitation clock

None, in the sense of a filing deadline running from a fixed date. The real clock here is procedural timing, not a limitation period: an objection to the *mode* of proof of an electronic record — that is, an objection that the certificate is missing or defective — must ordinarily be raised when the record is tendered, not for the first time in appeal, or it risks being treated as waived. An objection that the content is simply untrue, by contrast, is a matter for final arguments and is not waived by silence at the tendering stage. Keep these two objections, and their two different timings, strictly apart.

| Connects to | Provision | Why it matters |

| BSA | ss. 104–120 (burden of proof) | The burden of establishing the s. 63(2) conditions and producing a valid certificate lies on the party tendering the record, as with any other condition precedent to admissibility. |
| BNS 2023 | Offence provisions generally (e.g., forgery, cheating) | Electronic records certified under s. 63 are frequently the primary proof tendered for these offences — a WhatsApp confession, a forged-cheque CCTV clip, a phishing email trail. |
| BNSS 2023 | Provisions governing production and summoning of documents | Where a certificate cannot be obtained voluntarily, the *Arjun Panditrao* route to a court direction interlocks with BNSS's summons and production powers for the device or the certifying witness. |
| Application actually filed | Application for direction to produce a section 63(4) certificate, or to summon the certifying witness | The practical vehicle for invoking the non-cooperation exception described above. |

#### The authorities

**Anvar P.V. v. P.K. Basheer**, (2014) 10 SCC 473 — three-judge Bench. *Facts.* Election petition; secondary electronic evidence tendered without a s. 65B certificate. **Ratio.** Secondary evidence of an electronic record is inadmissible without the s. 65B(4) certificate; earlier observations suggesting an electronic record could alternatively be proved through ss. 63 and 65 of the old Act's general secondary-evidence scheme, without the certificate, do not represent the correct position. **Use.** The foundation case for treating the certificate as mandatory; cite alongside *Arjun Panditrao* for the current, settled position, not alone, since *Arjun Panditrao* is the more recent and more complete statement of the exceptions.

**State (NCT of Delhi) v. Navjot Sandhu**, (2005) 11 SCC 600. *Facts.* The Parliament-attack case; call records and intercepts tendered as secondary electronic evidence. **Ratio.** Contains observations, since disapproved, suggesting secondary electronic evidence could be admitted through the general secondary-evidence provisions even without a s. 65B certificate. **Use.** Cite only to show what the law used to allow and why *Anvar P.V.* and *Arjun Panditrao* were needed to correct it — never cite it today for the proposition that a certificate can be dispensed with.

#### Strategy and drafting

**The trap that gets electronic evidence excluded at the threshold.** Tendering the record and waiting to see if anyone objects, then scrambling for a certificate mid-trial. File the Schedule certificate — both parts, with the hash value — at the same time the record is first offered. A certificate produced after objection looks improvised even when it is accurate, and gives the other side a genuine, not merely tactical, basis to argue it was manufactured for the litigation.

**Timing.** Raise an objection to a missing or defective certificate the moment the record is tendered. Waiting until final arguments, or worse, until appeal, risks the objection being treated as waived, because this is an objection to *mode of proof*, which the limitation-clock block above requires to be raised contemporaneously.

**Whose signature, checked before filing, not after objection.** Confirm the Schedule certificate is signed by both the device custodian (Part A) and a person competent to speak as an expert (Part B) before it is filed — not after the other side has already flagged the gap.

**Model grounds for an objection to admissibility of an electronic record** (numbered so they can be copied directly into an objection memo):

1. No certificate under section 63(4) of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 has been filed along with the electronic record now sought to be tendered.
2. The certificate filed does not purport to be signed by a person in charge of the relevant computer or communication device, as required by section 63(4).
3. The certificate filed does not purport to be signed by an expert, as additionally required by section 63(4) and the Schedule to the Adhiniyam.
4. The certificate filed does not state a hash value of the electronic record, as required by Part A of the Schedule.
5. The certificate now relied upon was filed in respect of an earlier tender of the record at an earlier stage of these proceedings, and no fresh certificate has been filed for the present instance of tender, as required by section 63(4).
6. The conditions in section 63(2) are not addressed, or are not satisfied on the material on record, independently of any defect in the certificate.

## Chapter consolidation — does this electronic record even need a certificate at all?

Work through this in order before reaching for the Schedule form.

**Is the original device itself being produced, with its custodian deposing to it directly?** If yes, this is primary evidence and ss. 61 to 63 do not apply — go the ordinary route, and do not file a certificate that is not needed.

**Is it instead a printout, screenshot, CD, cloud export, or other copy?** Then it is computer output, and s. 63 is the only route. Confirm the four sub-section (2) conditions are actually satisfiable on the facts before spending time on the certificate — a device that was not regularly used for the relevant activity fails at the threshold regardless of paperwork.

**Has a Schedule-form certificate — both Part A and Part B, with a hash value — been prepared for *this specific tender*, at *this specific hearing*?** If a certificate exists but was filed at an earlier stage or in a different proceeding, it must be filed again. If no certificate exists and the natural custodian will not cooperate, document every request in writing now, so the *Arjun Panditrao* exception is available if it becomes necessary later.

**If all of the above are in order, admissibility follows — but admissibility is the beginning of the fight, not the end of it.** Genuineness of content is still open for argument at trial.
