# Chapter I — Preliminary

Two sections, and both do work far past their length. Section 1 fixes what the Adhiniyam covers and what it deliberately leaves alone — affidavits, arbitration. Section 2 is the Adhiniyam's own dictionary, and much of it is not "what does X mean" so much as "how confident must the Court be before treating X as settled" — the proved / disproved / not proved / may presume / shall presume / conclusive proof ladder that a large share of every later chapter quietly assumes the reader already has straight.

## Section 1 — Short title, application and commencement

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute Citing the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam to an arbitrator is citing the wrong law to the wrong forum.

Anita and Bharat are in a construction dispute referred to arbitration under their contract's arbitration clause. At a hearing before the sole arbitrator, Bharat's side tenders a site-inspection photograph without any certificate about the camera or device that produced it. Anita's advocate, Farhan, objects: "This electronic record is inadmissible — no section 63(4) certificate has been filed, as the Adhiniyam requires." The arbitrator overrules the objection in one line: this tribunal is not bound by the Adhiniyam at all.

Farhan is not wrong about what section 63(4) says. He is wrong about where it applies. Section 1 draws the line the rest of the Act assumes: judicial proceedings before a Court, yes; arbitration, no; affidavits, no. Getting this line wrong costs a hearing day and, worse, credibility with the forum in front of a client.
:::

::: story Being "not bound by the Act" is not the same as the Act being irrelevant to the forum.

Section 1(2) excludes arbitration and affidavits from the Adhiniyam's own force. It does not forbid an arbitrator or a Court reading an affidavit from finding the Act's concepts useful as a matter of practice — relevance, the weight of a document, what a certificate is for. Many arbitral tribunals follow evidence-law concepts by convention precisely because they are a sound way to weigh material, without being under any legal compulsion to do so.

The practical consequence is that an advocate before an excluded forum has no right to insist on the Adhiniyam's rules, and equally cannot be met with an objection framed only in the Adhiniyam's language, because the tribunal is not required to answer objections that way at all. Arguments there have to be framed as questions of fairness, reliability and the tribunal's own procedural rules — not as objections under a numbered section of an Act that, by its own text, does not reach that room.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

> **1.(1)** This Act [[!may be called]] the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023.
>
> **(2)** It [[!applies]] to all judicial proceedings in or before any Court, including Courts-martial, [[!but not to affidavits]] presented to any Court or officer, [[!nor to proceedings before an arbitrator]].
>
> **(3)** It [[!shall come into force]] on such date as the Central Government may, by notification in the Official Gazette, appoint.

**Statutory mechanics.**

| Operative expression | Character | Consequence |
|---|---|---|
| "applies to all judicial proceedings … including Courts-martial" | **Mandatory, inclusive** | The Adhiniyam binds every Court and every court-martial without exception — there is no discretion for a Court to opt out of it. |
| "but not to affidavits presented to any Court or officer" | **Mandatory exclusion** | An affidavit, though tendered to a Court, is not itself governed by the Adhiniyam's rules of evidence — it is a creature of procedural law (e.g. how a Court accepts affidavit evidence on an interlocutory application), not of this Act. |
| "nor to proceedings before an arbitrator" | **Mandatory exclusion** | Arbitral tribunals are outside the Adhiniyam entirely, not merely relaxed from some of its rules. |
| "shall come into force on such date … the Central Government may … appoint" | **Mandatory commencement mechanism** | The Act's operative date was fixed by notification, not by the date of assent — confirmed elsewhere in this project as **1 July 2024**. |

:::: proviso The arbitration carve-out is not this Act legislating modesty — it is confirmed from the other side too

Section 1(2) excludes arbitration from this Act's own force. The Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996, itself confirms the same boundary from the arbitration side: [[?an arbitral tribunal is not bound by]] the Code of Civil Procedure, 1908 or the Indian Evidence Act, 1872 (now, by necessary extension, the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam).

### In plain English

**Section 1(1) is a label, nothing more.** The short title lets every later section, and every other Act, refer to "the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023" without repeating its long description.

**Section 1(2) draws the boundary the entire rest of the Act assumes without repeating it.** It applies to every Court, expressly including courts-martial — so an argument that military justice is a separate system outside the Act's reach fails on the plain text. It excludes two things by name: affidavits presented to a Court or officer, and any proceeding before an arbitrator. Both exclusions matter for different reasons. Affidavits are excluded because they are treated procedurally, not as evidence proved live before the Court in the ordinary way. Arbitration is excluded because Parliament chose, consistently across the Arbitration and Conciliation Act, 1996 and this Act, to let arbitral tribunals set their own procedure for weighing material.

**Section 1(3) is why "when did the BSA start" is a real question and not a formality.** The Act was not self-commencing on the date of assent; it took effect only when the Central Government notified a date — 1 July 2024 — which matters enormously for any matter that straddles that date (see Chapter XII's repeal-and-savings provision for the transitional position, once that chapter is written).

### Test your instinct

::::instinct The arbitrator who is told to apply section 63

Anita's advocate, Farhan, objects before a sole arbitrator that Bharat's photograph lacks a section 63(4) certificate and is therefore inadmissible "as a matter of law."

Must the arbitrator exclude the photograph on that ground?

:::ruling Reveal the ruling
No. Section 1(2) excludes proceedings before an arbitrator from the Adhiniyam entirely. The arbitrator is free to consider the photograph, weigh it as he sees fit, and is not bound by section 63(4) or any other provision of this Act. Farhan's objection would carry weight before a Court; before this tribunal it carries none as a matter of law, though the arbitrator may still choose, as a matter of practice, to ask about the photograph's reliability.
::::

::::instinct The affidavit tendered as if it were oral testimony

At the final hearing of a suit — not an interlocutory application — Bharat's advocate tenders Bharat's affidavit alone as his entire evidence-in-chief, without calling him to the witness box.

Is the affidavit, by itself, evidence under the Adhiniyam?

:::ruling Reveal the ruling
No, not on its own force under this Act. Section 1(2) excludes affidavits presented to a Court from the Adhiniyam's own rules of evidence. Whether an affidavit can stand in for oral evidence-in-chief at a final hearing is governed by procedural law outside this Act (the Code of Civil Procedure's own provisions for affidavit evidence), not by anything in the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam — and a court applying those procedural rules will ordinarily still require the deponent to be offered for cross-examination before the affidavit carries evidentiary weight.

::::instinct The court-martial argument that looks clever and is wrong

An accused before a court-martial argues that military tribunals are a constitutionally separate system and that the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, being a civilian statute, does not bind them.

Does the argument succeed?

:::ruling Reveal the ruling
No. Section 1(2) says, in terms, that the Adhiniyam applies to all judicial proceedings "including Courts-martial." The argument sounds structurally appealing — separate system, separate procedure — but the text forecloses it directly. This is a clean example of an argument that is conceptually tidy and textually wrong; the plain words of the exclusion clause list only affidavits and arbitration, and a court-martial is not either.

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

Section 1 is a scope-of-application provision, not an admissibility gateway, so it does not carry a single dominant Supreme Court ratio the way sections 61 to 63 carry *Arjun Panditrao*. The closest settled authority on the general principle — that a body exercising purely administrative or domestic functions is not bound by the technical rules of the law of evidence and need only observe natural justice — is commonly cited as:

**Union of India v. T.R. Varma** — Supreme Court. *Facts, in one line.* A departmental disciplinary proceeding was challenged for not following the strict procedure of the Evidence Act. **Ratio.** A domestic or departmental tribunal is not bound by the technical rules of the Evidence Act; it must observe the principles of natural justice, but strict evidentiary procedure is not one of them. **Practitioner takeaway.** "This is a domestic tribunal, not a Court within the meaning of section 1 and section 2(1)(a) of the Adhiniyam; it is bound by natural justice, not by this Act's technical rules of proof."

The exact citation and bench strength for *T.R. Varma* are recalled from general legal knowledge and are not certain. Confirm against SCC Online or a similar primary source before relying on the precise citation in a filing.
:::

#### Sword and shield

| Used offensively (asserting the Act applies) | Used defensively (asserting the Act does not apply) |
|---|---|
| Show the proceeding is before a Court or court-martial exercising judicial functions, triggering the full weight of the Adhiniyam's rules. | Show the forum is an arbitral tribunal, or the material is an affidavit — both are expressly excluded by section 1(2). |
| Where a domestic tribunal purports to apply strict evidentiary technicalities to exclude your material, argue it need not — and should not — do so, since it is not a "Court" under section 2(1)(a). | Where a domestic tribunal or arbitrator is being pressed with a numbered objection under this Act, point out the objection has no legal force before that forum, though it may still be persuasive as a matter of practice. |
| Where the material is an affidavit at the final-hearing stage, insist on the deponent being offered for cross-examination before it is given evidentiary weight, since the Adhiniyam does not itself validate the affidavit as evidence. | — |

#### The limitation clock

None, in the sense of a filing deadline. The real timing point is different in kind: an objection that a forum has no power to apply the Adhiniyam at all (because it is an arbitral tribunal, for instance) is a jurisdictional objection, not a mode-of-proof objection, and is not waived merely by a party's silence at the time evidence is tendered — it can, in principle, be raised later, including on appeal or in a challenge to the award, because it goes to the forum's power rather than to the manner of proof.

#### The authorities

No further authorities are promoted for this section beyond the one discussed above; the "Court" versus "domestic tribunal" distinction is treated once here and should be linked to, not repeated, from later chapters (see the cross-reference logged in `TASKS.md`).

#### Strategy and drafting

**Do not cite this Act's numbered sections to a forum it does not bind.** An objection framed as "inadmissible under section 63(4)" before an arbitrator invites exactly the one-line dismissal in the opening scenario above. Frame the same substantive point — the material's reliability is unverified — in terms the forum actually answers to: its own procedural order, or general principles of fairness and reliability.

**Check the forum before drafting the objection, not after it is overruled.** Confirm, before the hearing, whether the tribunal is a Court, a court-martial, an arbitral tribunal, or a domestic/departmental body — the available objections differ completely across these four.

**Model grounds for a preliminary objection that the Adhiniyam does not bind this forum:**

1. The proceeding is before an arbitral tribunal constituted under an arbitration agreement, and section 1(2) of the Bharatiya Sakshya Adhiniyam, 2023 excludes proceedings before an arbitrator from this Act.
2. The material sought to be excluded is an affidavit presented to this Court/officer, and section 1(2) excludes affidavits from the Adhiniyam's own rules of evidence.
3. This is a departmental/domestic tribunal exercising administrative, not judicial, functions, and is accordingly not a "Court" within the meaning of section 2(1)(a) of the Adhiniyam.

## Section 2 — Definitions

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute "The Court shall presume" and "this is conclusive proof" sound like the same strength of statement. They are not, and the gap between them decides cases.

Anita relies on a provision elsewhere in the Adhiniyam that says the Court "shall presume" a fact in her favour. She tells her client the case is won. Bharat's advocate then leads evidence to rebut the presumed fact — and succeeds, because "shall presume" only shifts the burden onto Bharat; it does not forbid him from discharging it. Anita's advocate should have known the difference between a presumption that can be rebutted and one that, under a "conclusive proof" clause, cannot be touched by any amount of contrary evidence.
:::

::: story Section 2 is not a glossary to skim. It is a set of instructions for how hard the Court has to try before it accepts a fact.

Every later chapter uses these words as tools, not as ordinary English. "Proved," "disproved" and "not proved" are three different states a fact can be in at the end of a trial — not two. "May presume," "shall presume" and "conclusive proof" are three different strengths of statutory shortcut, from weakest to strongest, and confusing any two of them is the single most common way an otherwise sound argument collapses. Separately, and just as importantly, section 2 is where the Adhiniyam says out loud, for the first time in the history of this subject, that a WhatsApp message and a signed letter are both "documents" — a change that quietly licenses the entire electronic-records regime in sections 61 to 63.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

> **2.(1)** In this Adhiniyam, unless the context otherwise requires,—
>
> **(a)** [[=Court]] includes all Judges and Magistrates, and all persons, except arbitrators, legally authorised to take evidence;
>
> **(b)** [[=conclusive proof]] means when one fact is declared by this Adhiniyam to be conclusive proof of another, the Court [[!shall]], on proof of the one fact, regard the other as proved, and [[!shall not allow]] evidence to be given for the purpose of disproving it;
>
> **(c)** [[=disproved]] in relation to a fact, means when, after considering the matters before it, the Court either believes that it does not exist, or considers its non-existence so probable that a prudent man ought, under the circumstances of the particular case, to act upon the supposition that it does not exist;
>
> **(d)** [[=document]] means any matter expressed or described or otherwise recorded upon any substance by means of letters, figures or marks or any other means or by more than one of those means, intended to be used, or which may be used, for the purpose of recording that matter and [[!includes electronic and digital records]];
>
> **(e)** [[=evidence]] means and includes— (i) all statements including statements given electronically which the Court permits or requires to be made before it by witnesses in relation to matters of fact under inquiry and such statements are called oral evidence; (ii) all documents including electronic or digital records produced for the inspection of the Court and such documents are called documentary evidence;
>
> **(f)** [[=fact]] means and includes— (i) any thing, state of things, or relation of things, capable of being perceived by the senses; (ii) any mental condition of which any person is conscious;
>
> **(g)** [[=facts in issue]] means and includes any fact from which, either by itself or in connection with other facts, the existence, non-existence, nature or extent of any right, liability or disability, asserted or denied in any suit or proceeding, necessarily follows;
>
> **(h)** [[=may presume]].— Whenever it is provided by this Adhiniyam that the Court [[?may]] presume a fact, it may either regard such fact as proved, unless and until it is disproved, or may call for proof of it;
>
> **(i)** [[=not proved]].— A fact is said to be not proved when it is neither proved nor disproved;
>
> **(j)** [[=proved]].— A fact is said to be proved when, after considering the matters before it, the Court either believes it to exist, or considers its existence so probable that a prudent man ought, under the circumstances of the particular case, to act upon the supposition that it exists;
>
> **(k)** [[=relevant]].— A fact is said to be relevant to another when it is connected with the other in any of the ways referred to in the provisions of this Adhiniyam relating to the relevancy of facts;
>
> **(l)** [[=shall presume]].— Whenever it is directed by this Adhiniyam that the Court [[!shall]] presume a fact, it [[!shall]] regard such fact as proved, unless and until it is disproved.
>
> **(2)** Words and expressions used herein and not defined but defined in the Information Technology Act, 2000, the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 and the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 [[!shall have the same meanings]] as assigned to them in the said Act and Sanhitas.

**Statutory mechanics — the presumption ladder, weakest to strongest.**

| Term | Strength | What the Court must do |
|---|---|---|
| "may presume" | **Weakest — discretionary** | The Court may treat the fact as proved, or may instead call for further proof. Rebuttable, and not even automatically applied. |
| "shall presume" | **Middle — mandatory but rebuttable** | The Court must treat the fact as proved unless and until the opposing party disproves it. No discretion to ignore the presumption, but the opposing party can still defeat it with evidence. |
| "conclusive proof" | **Strongest — mandatory and irrebuttable** | The Court must treat the fact as proved and must not allow any evidence at all to be led against it. Not even good contrary evidence gets a hearing. |

**Statutory mechanics — the fact-finding trichotomy.**

| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| "proved" | The Court believes the fact exists, or finds its existence probable enough that a prudent person would act on that supposition. |
| "disproved" | The Court believes the fact does not exist, or finds its non-existence probable enough that a prudent person would act on that supposition. |
| "not proved" | Neither of the above — the evidence simply does not get the Court to either threshold. |

::: proviso "Document" and "evidence" now say the word "electronic" out loud — and that sentence is doing real work

Before this Act, whether a printout, an email, or a smartphone recording was a "document" at all rested on judicial interpretation of the old Act's general definition. Section 2(1)(d) closes that question by express words: "document" now expressly "includes electronic and digital records," and section 2(1)(e) says the same of "evidence," naming "statements given electronically" as oral evidence and "electronic or digital records" as documentary evidence. This is the definitional foundation the entire sections 61–63 certificate regime is built on — see [Sections 61 to 63](/chapters/chapter-05) for how a record that qualifies as a "document" under this section is actually admitted.
:::

### In plain English

**"Court" is narrower than the ordinary English word suggests.** Section 2(1)(a) includes Judges and Magistrates and anyone else legally authorised to take evidence — but expressly excludes arbitrators. Combined with section 1(2), this closes off any argument that an arbitral tribunal is a "Court" by another name.

**The presumption ladder is the single most consequential piece of vocabulary in the whole Act.** "May presume" is the weakest — a discretion, not an instruction. "Shall presume" is a mandatory but rebuttable instruction: the Court must start from the presumed fact, but the opposing party can still knock it down with evidence. "Conclusive proof" is the strongest and is not really a presumption at all — it is a statutory instruction to stop listening to contrary evidence altogether once the underlying fact is shown. Mistaking a "shall presume" clause for a "conclusive proof" clause, or vice versa, is the most common vocabulary error in this subject.

**"Proved," "disproved" and "not proved" are three states, not two.** A fact that is merely "not proved" has not been affirmatively disbelieved — it has simply failed to reach either threshold. Whether that helps or hurts a party depends entirely on who carried the burden of proof for that fact, which is why this trichotomy has to be read together with Chapter VII once that chapter is written.

**"Document" and "evidence" now name electronic and digital records directly**, rather than leaving the question to case law built on an older, paper-oriented definition. The practical payoff of this definitional change is worked through in full in the sections 61–63 admissibility gateway.

**"Fact," "facts in issue" and "relevant" are the vocabulary the whole of Chapter II is built on.** "Fact" includes not only physical things and events but also a person's mental state where consciously held. "Facts in issue" are the facts a suit or proceeding is actually about — what a plaint or charge asserts and the other side denies. "Relevant" is deliberately circular within this Act: a fact is relevant only if some other provision of the Adhiniyam (chiefly Chapter II) says it is connected to another fact in one of the ways that Chapter recognises — relevance is not a matter of general logic, it is a matter of falling within a named category.

**Delta bucket, stated definition by definition, because this section does not fall in one bucket.**

- **"Court," the presumption ladder (may presume / shall presume / conclusive proof), and the proved / disproved / not proved trichotomy — renumbered only.** These appear unchanged in substance from the old Act's interpretation clause, now organised into labelled paragraphs instead of continuous prose.
- **"Document" and "evidence" — substantively amended, and confirmed, not merely hypothesised.** This project's own roadmap (`reference/05-bsa-roadmap.md`) flagged, as an unverified hypothesis, that BSA might fold electronic and digital records directly into its core definitions rather than relying on special provisions alone. Having now read section 2(1)(d) and (e) directly, that hypothesis is **confirmed**: both definitions name "electronic and digital records" and "statements given electronically" expressly, which the 1872 Act's definition of "document" did not.
- **Section 2(2) — new machinery, not present in the old Act in this form.** A cross-reference clause borrowing definitions from the Information Technology Act, 2000, the Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023, and the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 for any term this Act uses but does not itself define. This is a structural change: the Adhiniyam is drafted to be read together with its two sibling 2023 codes for vocabulary, not as a freestanding glossary.

**Side by side, where the text actually changed.**

| IEA 1872, s. 3 ("document") | BSA 2023, s. 2(1)(d) ("document") |
|---|---|
| "…any matter expressed or described upon any substance by means of letters, figures or marks…intended to be used, or which may be used, for the purpose of recording that matter." | "…any matter expressed or described **or otherwise recorded** upon any substance by means of letters, figures or marks **or any other means** or by more than one of those means…and **includes electronic and digital records**." |

**What a practitioner must now do differently.** Stop arguing, as a threshold matter, that an email, a chat log, or a voice note is not a "document" at all — section 2(1)(d) closes that argument outright. The live fight has moved entirely to the sections 61–63 admissibility gateway: whether the record satisfies the certificate and cumulative-conditions test, not whether it counts as a document in the first place.

::::instinct The "shall presume" that a client thought meant automatic victory

A provision elsewhere in the Adhiniyam directs that the Court "shall presume" a particular fact once a foundational fact is shown. Anita establishes the foundational fact and tells her client the point is won. Bharat then leads clear, credible evidence rebutting the presumed fact.

Does Bharat's rebuttal evidence get heard at all?

:::ruling Reveal the ruling
Yes. "Shall presume," under section 2(1)(l), is mandatory but rebuttable — the Court must start from the presumed fact, but the opposing party remains free to disprove it with evidence, and if that evidence succeeds, the presumption falls. Anita's client was told the case was "won" on the strength of a presumption that only ever shifted the burden onto Bharat; it never took the question out of contest the way a "conclusive proof" clause would have.
::::

::::instinct The genuinely persuasive evidence a "conclusive proof" clause will not even hear

A different provision elsewhere in the Adhiniyam declares a particular certified fact to be "conclusive proof" of another. Bharat has strong, credible evidence contradicting the certified fact — an expert report, contemporaneous documents, all pointing the other way.

Is Bharat's contrary evidence admitted and weighed?

:::ruling Reveal the ruling
No — and Bharat loses this point regardless of how strong his contrary evidence actually is. Section 2(1)(b) defines "conclusive proof" precisely so that the Court "shall not allow evidence to be given for the purpose of disproving it." This is not a comment on the quality of Bharat's evidence; the Court never reaches that question, because a "conclusive proof" clause forecloses the rebuttal enquiry entirely once the underlying fact is shown. This is the honest, uncomfortable edge of the presumption ladder: the strongest tier is strong precisely because it refuses to listen, not because the contrary evidence is weak.

:::ruling Reveal the ruling
Yes. Section 2(1)(d) expressly includes "electronic and digital records" within the definition of "document," and section 2(1)(e) separately treats electronic or digital records as documentary evidence. Whether the voice note is actually *admitted* is a different question entirely, governed by the sections 61–63 gateway (the device it was recorded and stored on, the certificate, the cumulative conditions) — but the threshold argument that it is not a document at all fails outright.

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio

Section 2 defines vocabulary rather than creating an independent right or gateway, so — as with section 1 — no single Supreme Court decision construes "section 2" as such the way *Arjun Panditrao* construes sections 61 to 63. The most useful illustration of how the presumption ladder actually operates in real litigation comes from a different statute's own "shall presume" clause, cited here as illustration of the *mechanism* this section defines, not as authority on this Act:

**Rangappa v. Sri Mohan** — Supreme Court. *Facts, in one line.* A cheque-dishonour prosecution turned on the presumption of a legally enforceable debt under section 139 of the Negotiable Instruments Act, 1881 — itself a "shall presume" clause of the same statutory character section 2(1)(l) defines. **Ratio.** A "shall presume" clause raises a rebuttable presumption of law; the accused need only establish a probable defence on a preponderance of probabilities to displace it, not prove the contrary beyond reasonable doubt. **Practitioner takeaway.** "A 'shall presume' clause shifts the evidential burden; it does not demand proof beyond reasonable doubt to rebut, and it is not a 'conclusive proof' clause in disguise."

#### Sword and shield

| Used offensively (relying on a definition) | Used defensively (resisting reliance on a definition) |
|---|---|
| Characterise a favourable statutory statement as "conclusive proof" wherever the text actually supports it, to shut down the opponent's rebuttal evidence before it is even offered. | Characterise the same statement as merely "may presume" or "shall presume," wherever the text supports the weaker reading, to keep the door open for rebuttal evidence. |
| Invoke section 2(1)(d) and (e) directly to defeat a bare "this is not a document" objection to electronic material. | Concede the record is a "document" but redirect the fight to the sections 61–63 admissibility gateway, which is the argument actually capable of succeeding. |
| Argue a fact is merely "not proved," not "disproved," where the burden of proof lay on the opponent — the distinction can decide who wins without either side affirmatively disproving anything. | Argue the opponent has confused "not proved" with "disproved" to claim a stronger win than the record actually supports. |

#### The limitation clock

None. This is a definitions section; it fixes no deadline of its own. A legal argument that a party or a lower court has mischaracterised a statutory word — treating a "may presume" clause as though it were "conclusive proof," for instance — is an error of law that can, in principle, be raised at any stage, including in appeal, because it goes to the correct legal characterisation of a provision rather than to the timing of an evidentiary objection.

#### Strategy and drafting

**Read every presumption clause you plan to rely on, or resist, for its exact word before pleading anything about it.** "May presume," "shall presume" and "conclusive proof" look similar in a hurried read and are not interchangeable in argument — the entire strength of a submission can turn on which one the drafter actually used.

**Do not concede "not a document" arguments away for free, but do not stop there either.** Section 2(1)(d) and (e) settle the document/evidence threshold question for electronic material outright; spend the argument's real energy on the sections 61–63 gateway instead, since that is where these cases are actually won or lost.

**Model grounds distinguishing presumption strength (for use in a written submission):**

1. The provision relied upon by the opposing party uses the words "may presume," which under section 2(1)(h) confers a discretion on the Court, not a mandatory instruction, and is in any event rebuttable.
2. The provision relied upon uses "shall presume," which under section 2(1)(l) is mandatory but remains rebuttable by evidence, which the opposing party has failed to lead / has successfully led (as applicable).
3. The fact in question has, on the evidence, reached neither the "proved" threshold under section 2(1)(j) nor the "disproved" threshold under section 2(1)(c), and is accordingly "not proved" under section 2(1)(i), with the consequence that [the party bearing the burden] has failed to discharge it.

## Chapter consolidation — does the Adhiniyam even apply here, and which vocabulary word governs this fact?

Work through this in order.

**Is the forum a Court (including a court-martial), or is it an arbitral tribunal, a domestic/departmental body, or is the material an affidavit?** If the forum is excluded or the material is an affidavit, section 1(2) means the Adhiniyam does not bind it — reframe the argument in terms the forum actually answers to.

**If the Adhiniyam does apply, which presumption word, if any, governs the fact in question?** "May presume" is a discretion; "shall presume" is mandatory but rebuttable; "conclusive proof" forecloses rebuttal entirely. Confusing any two of these is the most common vocabulary error in this subject, and it decides real cases.

**If the material in question is electronic or digital, section 2(1)(d) and (e) have already answered "is this a document" — yes.** The live fight is the sections 61–63 admissibility gateway, not the definitional threshold.
