# Chapter VI — Of the Exclusion of Oral Evidence by Documentary Evidence (ss. 94-103)

Chapter VI is short in section count — ten sections — but dense in substance: it is the Act's parol evidence rule, the doctrine that once parties have put their bargain into a document, oral evidence generally cannot be used to contradict, add to, or explain away what the document says. Three clusters carry it: ss. 94-95 state the exclusion itself, with section 95's six provisos doing most of the real work; ss. 96-101 supply six rules for how a document's language is to be construed when it is ambiguous, ill-fitting, or technical; ss. 102-103 close with a third-party carve-out and a savings clause for wills.

## Sections 94 to 95 — the exclusion itself, and its six provisos

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute
Two parties sign a written contract. Later, one of them wants to testify that they had also orally agreed to something different, or something extra, that the document does not mention. Can the court hear that oral evidence at all? Section 94 says: once the terms of a contract, grant, or other property disposition have been reduced to a document (or the law requires them to be), the document itself — or admissible secondary evidence of it — is the only proof of its terms; no oral evidence of those terms is permitted. Section 95 goes further: no oral evidence may be given to contradict, vary, add to, or subtract from a document's proved terms, subject to six carefully carved-out provisos that let some oral evidence back in.
:::

::: story
The rule exists because written documents are what the parties chose as the fixed record of their bargain — allowing either side to later swear to a different oral version would let litigation rewrite the deal after the fact. But the rule cannot be absolute: fraud, mistake, and genuinely separate agreements are real, and the six provisos are the Act's considered list of where oral evidence is still allowed in without undermining the document's primacy.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

> **94.** When the terms of a contract, or of a grant, or of any other disposition of property, have been reduced to the form of a document, and in all cases in which any matter is required by law to be reduced to the form of a document, [[!no evidence shall be given]] in proof of the terms of such contract, grant or other disposition of property, or of such matter, except the document itself, or secondary evidence of its contents in cases in which secondary evidence is admissible under the provisions hereinbefore contained.
>
> Exception 1.—When a public officer is required by law to be appointed in writing, and when it is shown that any particular person has acted as such officer, the writing by which he is appointed need not be proved.
>
> Exception 2.—Wills admitted to probate in India may be proved by the probate.
>
> Explanation 1.—This section applies equally to cases in which the contracts, grants or dispositions of property referred to are contained in one document, and to cases in which they are contained in more documents than one.
>
> Explanation 2.—Where there are more originals than one, one original only need be proved.
>
> Explanation 3.—The statement, in any document whatever, of a fact other than the facts referred to in this section, shall not preclude the admission of oral evidence as to the same fact.
>
> **95.** When the terms of any such contract, grant or other disposition of property, or any matter required by law to be reduced to the form of a document, have been proved according to section 94, [[!no evidence]] of any oral agreement or statement shall be admitted, as between the parties to any such instrument or their representatives in interest, for the purpose of contradicting, varying, adding to, or subtracting from, its terms:
>
> Provided that any fact may be proved which would invalidate any document, or which would entitle any person to any decree or order relating thereto; such as fraud, intimidation, illegality, want of due execution, want of capacity in any contracting party, want or failure of consideration, or mistake in fact or law:
>
> Provided further that the existence of any separate oral agreement as to any matter on which a document is silent, and which is not inconsistent with its terms, may be proved. In considering whether or not this proviso applies, the Court shall have regard to the degree of formality of the document:
>
> Provided also that the existence of any separate oral agreement, constituting a condition precedent to the attaching of any obligation under any such contract, grant or disposition of property, may be proved:
>
> Provided also that the existence of any distinct subsequent oral agreement to rescind or modify any such contract, grant or disposition of property, may be proved, except in cases in which such contract, grant or disposition of property is by law required to be in writing, or has been registered according to the law in force for the time being as to the registration of documents:
>
> Provided also that any usage or custom by which incidents not expressly mentioned in any contract are usually annexed to contracts of that description, may be proved:
>
> Provided also that the annexing of such incident would not be repugnant to, or inconsistent with, the express terms of the contract:
>
> Provided also that any fact may be proved which shows in what manner the language of a document is related to existing facts.

| Text | What it does | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| S. 94 | Bars oral evidence of the terms of a document once the parties' bargain has been reduced to writing (or the law required it to be), unless secondary evidence is separately admissible under ss. 58-60 | The document itself becomes the exclusive proof of its own terms — this is the exclusion's threshold rule |
| S. 94, Exceptions 1-2 | Excuses proof of a public officer's appointment-in-writing once the officer is shown to have acted, and lets a probated will be proved by the probate itself | Narrow, practical carve-outs where re-proving the underlying document would be pointless or duplicative |
| S. 94, Explanations 1-3 | Clarifies that the rule applies whether the bargain sits in one document or several (all must be proved), that only one original need be proved where several originals exist, and that a document's incidental statement of an unrelated fact does not block oral evidence of that unrelated fact | Prevents the exclusion from being read more broadly than it is meant to reach |
| S. 95 | Once a document's terms are proved under s. 94, bars oral evidence between the parties (or their representatives) that would contradict, vary, add to, or subtract from those terms | This is the operative parol evidence rule — it protects the proved document's terms from being reopened by oral testimony |
| S. 95, six provisos | Preserve oral evidence of: (1) facts invalidating the document or founding a decree (fraud, mistake, etc.); (2) a separate oral agreement on a matter the document is silent on and not inconsistent with it; (3) a separate oral condition precedent; (4) a distinct subsequent oral agreement to rescind or modify (unless writing/registration was legally required); (5) usage or custom annexing unstated incidents, if not repugnant to the document's express terms; (6) facts showing how the document's language relates to existing facts | These are the Act's own considered list of where the exclusion in s. 95 does not reach — a fact pattern must fit one of the six precisely, not merely feel fair, to let oral evidence back in |

::: proviso Six gateways back into oral evidence — not one general "fairness" exception
Section 95's bar is not absolute, but the doors back in are specific and closed-list, not an open invitation for "the oral evidence that would have been fair to admit." A party seeking to introduce oral evidence about a documented bargain must identify which of the six provisos the evidence actually fits — invalidating fact, silent-and-consistent separate agreement, condition precedent, subsequent rescission/modification, annexed usage or custom, or language-to-facts context — rather than arguing generally that excluding the evidence would be unjust.
:::

### In plain English

**Once a bargain is on paper, the paper is the story — unless the oral evidence fits one of six named exceptions.** Section 94 says the document itself, not a witness's memory of the negotiations, proves what a written contract or property disposition actually says. Section 95 then says that even the parties themselves cannot use oral testimony to rewrite, expand, or shrink the document's proved terms after the fact.

But the Act does not pretend documents capture everything real about a transaction. The six provisos are practical safety valves: they let in evidence of fraud or mistake (because a fraudulently or mistakenly documented "agreement" was never a real meeting of minds); a genuinely separate oral agreement on a topic the document simply never addresses; a spoken condition that had to be satisfied before the document's obligations even started; a later oral deal to undo or change the arrangement (subject to registration/writing requirements); customary incidents ordinarily attached to that kind of contract; and evidence that simply explains how the document's words connect to the real-world facts. Each is narrow and specific — none is a general licence to explain away an inconvenient document.

### Test your instinct

::::instinct Scenario 1 — the receipt that does not tell the whole story
A gives B a written receipt reciting that B paid a fixed sum for goods. B later wants to prove orally that he actually paid a different, larger sum, because the receipt understates it. Can B lead this oral evidence?
:::ruling
Yes, on facts of this kind — the Act's own illustration to section 94 treats a receipt as not exhaustively fixing the fact of payment for section 94 purposes, so oral evidence of the actual payment is not automatically barred as it would be for a contract's substantive terms. This is a narrow illustration-driven point, not a general licence to contradict any written acknowledgment; it must genuinely fit the same pattern the Act's own illustration draws.
:::
::::

::::instinct Scenario 2 — the tenant who orally negotiated free board
A signs a formal, advocate-drafted, stamped lease for lodgings for a year. The lease is silent on board (meals). A wants to testify that, orally, board was agreed to be included. Can he?
:::ruling
It depends heavily on the document's formality — which the Act expressly tells the court to weigh under the second proviso. A brief, informal card reciting only the rent is treated differently from a formal, advocate-drafted, stamped agreement: the more formal and complete the document appears, the less likely a court is to accept that a significant term like included board was simply left out by oversight rather than deliberately excluded. On facts resembling a fully formal instrument, A's oral evidence of included board is unlikely to succeed.
:::
::::

::::instinct Scenario 3 — the induced mining contract
A enters a written contract with B to work B's mines on certain terms, induced by B's misrepresentation about the mines' value. A wants to prove the misrepresentation orally to avoid the contract. Does section 95 bar this?
:::ruling
No. The first proviso to section 95 expressly preserves any fact that would invalidate the document — and misrepresentation inducing a contract is exactly that kind of fact. A may lead oral evidence of the misrepresentation notwithstanding the general bar, because doing so does not seek to vary the contract's terms; it seeks to invalidate the contract altogether.
:::
::::

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio
No single authority is promoted here. The parol evidence rule in ss. 94-95 is worked out almost entirely through the Act's own text, Explanations, and its unusually rich set of illustrations.

#### Sword and shield
| | As a sword (offering evidence) | As a shield (resisting evidence) |
|---|---|---|
| S. 94 | Rely on the document itself (or properly admissible secondary evidence) as sufficient, exclusive proof of the terms, without needing corroborating oral testimony | Object to any oral evidence of a documented term's content that does not fit an Exception |
| S. 95 | Identify precisely which of the six provisos an inconvenient oral fact fits, before attempting to lead it | Meet each proviso argument head-on by showing the oral evidence in truth seeks to contradict or vary a term, not merely to invalidate, supplement, condition, rescind, customarily annex, or contextualise it |

#### The limitation clock
None of its own. As elsewhere in this chapter, an objection to oral evidence offered in breach of ss. 94-95 should be raised when that evidence is tendered, not left until final arguments.

#### Interlocking matrix
| This provision | Interlocks with | Why |
|---|---|---|
| S. 94 | Ch. V part I, ss. 56-60 (`chapter-05a.md`) | S. 94's reference to "secondary evidence... admissible under the provisions hereinbefore contained" is a direct pointer to the ss. 58-60 secondary-evidence gateways — the two must be read together, not in isolation. |
| S. 95, first proviso | Ch. II part II, ss. 22-24 (`chapter-02b.md`) and Ch. #### The authorities
No confidently recalled leading authority is promoted for this cluster.

#### Strategy and drafting
Before tendering any oral evidence about a documented bargain, identify affirmatively which of section 95's six provisos it fits and be ready to say so on the record when objection is taken — a vague appeal to fairness will not survive a focused objection. When resisting such evidence, test the document's degree of formality explicitly, since the Act itself makes that the deciding factor for the second proviso, and press for the document to be produced and marked before any oral variation is even discussed.

## Sections 96 to 101 — construing ambiguous, misapplied, and technical language

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute
A document's words do not always map cleanly onto the world. Sometimes the words themselves are ambiguous or incomplete; sometimes they are perfectly clear but simply do not fit any real-world fact; sometimes they are clear but fit facts in an unintended way; sometimes they could fit any one of several things but not more than one; sometimes they partly fit two different sets of facts; and sometimes they use technical, foreign, obsolete, or illegible terms that need outside evidence just to be read at all. Sections 96 to 101 give the court six distinct rules for exactly when extrinsic evidence may, or may not, be used to work out what a document's language actually means.
:::

::: story
The line these six sections draw is between using evidence to identify what already-plain-and-fitting words mean, which is not allowed, and using evidence to work out which of several possible meanings, applications, or referents the words were meant to carry, which is allowed. The document's language is never rewritten by outside evidence — but where the language genuinely leaves a real-world question open, evidence may answer that question.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

> **96.** When the language used in a document is, on its face, ambiguous or defective, [[!evidence may not be given]] of facts which would show its meaning or supply its defects.
>
> **97.** When language used in a document is plain in itself, and when it applies accurately to existing facts, [[!evidence may not be given]] to show that it was not meant to apply to such facts.
>
> **98.** When language used in a document is plain in itself, but is unmeaning in reference to existing facts, [[?evidence may be given]] to show that it was used in a peculiar sense.
>
> **99.** When the facts are such that the language used might have been meant to apply to any one, and could not have been meant to apply to more than one, of several persons or things, [[?evidence may be given]] of facts which show which of those persons or things it was intended to apply to.
>
> **100.** When the language used applies partly to one set of existing facts, and partly to another set of existing facts, but the whole of it does not apply correctly to either, [[?evidence may be given]] to show to which of the two it was meant to apply.
>
> **101.** [[?Evidence may be given]] to show the meaning of illegible or not commonly intelligible characters, of foreign, obsolete, technical, local and regional expressions, of abbreviations and of words used in a peculiar sense.

| Text | What it does | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| S. 96 | Bars evidence to fix a document's meaning where the language is, on its face, ambiguous or defective | A genuinely ambiguous or incomplete document cannot be rescued by outside evidence supplying what the drafter left unclear — the ambiguity simply stands |
| S. 97 | Bars evidence to show plain, accurately-fitting language was not meant to apply, once it does accurately fit the facts | Prevents a party from using extrinsic evidence to escape language that says exactly what it says and fits exactly what it describes |
| S. 98 | Permits evidence to show plain language was used in a peculiar sense, where that language is otherwise unmeaning against the actual facts | Lets evidence resolve a real mismatch between plain words and reality, rather than leaving the document meaningless |
| S. 99 | Permits evidence to identify which one of several possible persons or things the language was meant to apply to, where it could fit exactly one but not more than one | Resolves a targeting ambiguity — which of several candidates was meant — without rewriting the words themselves |
| S. 100 | Permits evidence to show which of two partially-matching fact sets the language was meant to apply to, where neither fits perfectly | Handles the case where the words are a genuine hybrid description fitting neither candidate exactly |
| S. 101 | Permits evidence of the meaning of illegible, unintelligible, foreign, obsolete, technical, local, regional, abbreviated, or peculiarly-used language | Recognises that some language cannot be read or understood at all without outside help, quite apart from any ambiguity in what it refers to |

::: proviso Plain-and-fitting is untouchable; ambiguous, unfitting, or unintelligible is not
The dividing line across ss. 96-101 is this: where language is plain on its face and accurately fits the facts, sections 96 and 97 shut the door on evidence altogether — the document is taken exactly at its word. Every other section in this cluster (98, 99, 100, 101) opens the door precisely because something is genuinely wrong on the face of it: the words do not fit, could fit several things, partly fit two things, or cannot be read or understood without outside help. Evidence is never used under ss. 98-101 to override language that is both plain and accurately fitting — that is exactly what ss. 96-97 forbid.
:::

### In plain English

**Two sections shut the door; four open it — and the difference is whether the words themselves are doing their job.** If a document's language is genuinely unclear on its face (section 96), or if it is clear and fits the facts perfectly (section 97), the Act will not let evidence step in to fix the first problem or escape the second outcome. But where the words are clear yet simply do not fit anything real (section 98), could fit more than one real candidate but only one was meant (section 99), partially describe two different things without matching either exactly (section 100), or are written in language that cannot be read or understood without help — illegible writing, obsolete or technical terms, foreign words, abbreviations (section 101) — evidence is allowed in specifically to answer that narrow question. The illustrations make the pattern concrete: "my white horse" when there are two white horses needs section 99's kind of evidence; "my house in Kolkata" when the seller only ever had a house in Howrah needs section 98's.

### Test your instinct

::::instinct Scenario 1 — the price with two figures
A written agreement to sell a horse states the price as "one lakh rupees or one lakh fifty thousand rupees." The buyer wants to lead evidence of pre-contract discussions to show which figure was agreed. Is this evidence admissible?
:::ruling
No. This is precisely section 96's territory — the language is ambiguous on its face (it names two alternative prices with no mechanism to choose between them), and evidence to show which one was meant, or to supply the missing choice, is barred. The ambiguity is left standing; the remedy, if any, lies outside the document's construction rules entirely.
:::
::::

::::instinct Scenario 2 — "my estate at Rampur" that fits exactly
A deed conveys "my estate at Rampur containing one hundred bighas." The seller in fact owns exactly one estate at Rampur of exactly that size. The seller later claims he actually meant to sell a different, smaller estate elsewhere. Can he lead evidence of that intention?
:::ruling
No. Section 97 squarely bars this. The language is plain and accurately fits an existing fact — the seller's one Rampur estate of that exact size — so evidence that something different was "really" meant cannot be given. The document's plain, fitting words control.
:::
::::

::::instinct Scenario 3 — "my house in Kolkata" that does not exist
A deed conveys "my house in Kolkata." The seller never owned a house in Kolkata, but did own a house in Howrah, which the buyer took possession of immediately after the deed was executed. Can evidence be given that "Kolkata" was a mistake for "Howrah"?
:::ruling
Yes. This is section 98's exact illustration: the language is plain in itself but unmeaning against the actual facts, since no Kolkata house exists to which it could refer. Evidence — including the buyer's immediate, apparently accepted possession of the Howrah house — may be given to show the deed was meant, in a peculiar sense, to refer to that property.
:::
::::

## Sections 102 to 103 — the third-party carve-out and the wills savings clause

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute
Sections 94-95's bar on oral evidence protects the parties to a document and their representatives in interest from having the document's terms reopened by oral testimony — but what about someone who was never a party to it at all, and whose own interests are affected by what really happened? Section 102 answers: a stranger to the document may give evidence of a contemporaneous oral agreement varying its terms, even though the parties themselves could not. Section 103 closes the chapter with a narrow savings clause: nothing here disturbs the Indian Succession Act, 1925's own rules for construing wills.
:::

::: story
The parol evidence rule protects the parties' own bargain from being rewritten by their own later say-so — but a third party never made that bargain and never agreed to be bound by the document's four corners. Letting a stranger prove the real, contemporaneous arrangement where it affects that stranger's own interest does not undermine the document between the people who actually signed it.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

> **102.** Persons who are not parties to a document, or their representatives in interest, [[?may give evidence]] of any facts tending to show a contemporaneous agreement varying the terms of the document.
>
> **103.** Nothing in this Chapter shall be taken to affect any of the provisions of the Indian Succession Act, 1925 as to the construction of wills.

| Text | What it does | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| S. 102 | Lets a non-party (or their representative in interest) give evidence of a contemporaneous agreement varying a document's terms, even though the parties themselves could not | Recognises that the exclusionary bar exists to protect the parties' own choice of a written record, not to blind the court to the real facts affecting a genuine stranger's interests |
| S. 103 | Expressly preserves the Indian Succession Act, 1925's own construction rules for wills, unaffected by anything in this Chapter | Avoids an unintended clash between this Chapter's general document-construction rules and the specialised, separate statutory scheme for interpreting wills |

::: proviso Section 102 only ever helps the stranger, never the parties themselves
Section 102 does not create a general loophole around sections 94-95 for the contracting parties — "as between A and B," the Act's own illustration says, the contemporaneous oral agreement still cannot be shown by A or B. It is C, a genuine stranger whose own interest is affected, who may lead that evidence. A party looking to escape the exclusionary bar cannot borrow section 102 by finding a friendly third party to speak on their behalf if the substance of the evidence is really the parties' own attempt to vary their own document.
:::

### In plain English

**A stranger to the document is not bound by the document's own rule against contradicting itself.** Sections 94-95 protect the parties' choice to reduce their bargain to writing — but that protection runs between the parties, not to the whole world. Someone who was never a party, and whose own interest is genuinely affected by what really happened, may prove the real, contemporaneous arrangement even though the signatories themselves are stuck with what the document says. Section 103, by contrast, is a pure housekeeping provision: it makes sure this Chapter's general document-construction machinery does not accidentally override the separate, specialised rules the Indian Succession Act, 1925 already supplies for reading wills.

### Test your instinct

::::instinct Scenario 1 — the third party affected by a hidden oral credit term
A and B sign a written cotton-supply contract silent on credit terms, but orally agree, at the same time, that A gets three months' credit. C, a creditor of A's whose recovery depends on when A's debt to B falls due, wants to prove this oral credit arrangement. Can C do so, even though A and B themselves could not have shown it against each other on these facts?
:::ruling
Yes, if the arrangement genuinely affects C's own interest — this is section 102's own illustration. The bar in sections 94-95 runs between A and B as parties to the document; it does not prevent C, a stranger whose recovery is affected, from proving the real contemporaneous arrangement, even on facts where A and B themselves would be bound by the document's silence.
:::
::::

::::instinct Scenario 2 — construing an ambiguous clause in a will
An executor wants to argue that a will's ambiguous clause should be read using this Chapter's section 96 exclusionary approach, rather than the Indian Succession Act's own construction principles, because the Chapter's rule is more favourable to her position. Does section 103 allow this choice?
:::ruling
No. Section 103 expressly reserves the Indian Succession Act, 1925's own rules for construing wills — this Chapter's rules, including section 96, do not apply to that task at all where the Succession Act's own scheme governs. The executor cannot pick and choose the more favourable regime; wills are construed under their own dedicated statutory rules, untouched by this Chapter.
:::
::::

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio
No single authority is promoted here. Both sections are narrow, self-contained rules worked out through the Act's own text and its single illustration.

#### Sword and shield
| | As a sword (offering evidence) | As a shield (resisting evidence) |
|---|---|---|
| S. 102 | If genuinely a non-party with an affected interest, lead the contemporaneous oral agreement directly rather than through the document's own signatories | Challenge whether the person offering the evidence is truly a stranger, or is in substance a party or representative attempting to evade section 95 |
| S. 103 | For will-construction disputes, argue exclusively from the Indian Succession Act, 1925's own rules | Resist any attempt to import this Chapter's exclusionary rules (particularly ss. 96-97) into a will-construction dispute |

#### The limitation clock
None of its own for either section.

#### Interlocking matrix
| This provision | Interlocks with | Why |
|---|---|---|
| S. 102 | Ss. 94-95 (this file, above) | Section 102 is best understood as the express limit on who ss. 94-95's exclusionary bar actually binds — parties and their representatives, not the whole world — and should be read immediately alongside them, not as a free-standing rule. |
| S. 103 | Indian Succession Act, 1925 | Descriptive-only cross-reference; no verified local Indian Succession Act source exists in `reference/` — if one is ever added, confirm the actual will-construction provisions this section defers to. |

#### The authorities
No confidently recalled leading authority is promoted for this cluster.

#### Strategy and drafting
When relying on section 102, establish the witness's non-party status and genuine affected interest clearly on the record before leading the contemporaneous-agreement evidence, since the opposing side's most obvious objection will be that the witness is a party in substance. In any will dispute, plead the Indian Succession Act's own construction provisions from the outset rather than this Chapter's rules, to avoid a threshold objection under section 103.

## Chapter VI consolidation — reading the parol evidence rule as one whole

1. **Has the transaction's terms been reduced to a document, engaging section 94 at all?** If not, this Chapter's exclusion never begins.
2. **Is oral evidence being offered to contradict, vary, add to, or subtract from the document's proved terms, and does it fit one of section 95's six provisos?** If it does not fit one of the six, it is excluded regardless of how fair it might otherwise seem.
3. **Is the dispute really about what the document's words mean or apply to, rather than about varying its terms?** If so, move to sections 96-101 and identify precisely which of the six construction rules the language problem fits — plain-and-fitting and on-its-face-ambiguous language get no help from evidence; peculiar-sense, several-candidates, partial-fit, and unintelligible language do.
4. **Is the person offering the evidence a genuine stranger to the document, or is a will involved?** If a genuine stranger, section 102 may free the evidence from the parties' own bar; if a will, section 103 hands the whole inquiry over to the Indian Succession Act, 1925 instead.
