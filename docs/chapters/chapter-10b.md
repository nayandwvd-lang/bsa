# Chapter X part II — Of Examination of Witnesses: document contents through a witness, and the Court's control over credit-shaking questions (ss. 147-155)

This second part of Chapter X covers two connected problems in cross-examination practice. The first cluster deals with proving the contents of a document through a witness's oral testimony, and the closely related rule on cross-examining a witness about their own prior written statements. The second cluster deals with the much-litigated boundary of "lawful" cross-examination — questions that test veracity, identity, or credit even at the cost of some indignity to the witness — and the layered set of controls the Court retains over questions that serve no purpose but to injure a witness's character on matters not otherwise relevant to the case.

## Sections 147 to 150 — proving document contents through a witness, and the lawful limits of cross-examination

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute
A witness starts describing, in words, what a written contract said — without the contract itself ever being produced. Section 147 gives the adverse party a tool to stop this: object to the oral evidence until the document is produced, or until the party calling the witness proves facts entitling them to give secondary evidence of it instead. This does not bar oral evidence of statements *about* a document's contents that are independently relevant (its own Explanation and illustration make this precise). Section 148 then addresses cross-examining a witness on their own prior written statement — permitted freely without even showing the writing to the witness, unless the cross-examiner intends to contradict the witness with it, in which case the relevant parts must first be pointed out. Section 149 lists what a cross-examiner may lawfully ask even at the cost of exposing the witness to self-incrimination or forfeiture — veracity, identity, and credit-shaking questions — subject to a specific statutory carve-out protecting victims of certain sexual offences from being cross-examined on general immoral character or past sexual history to prove consent. Section 150 then ties credit-relevant questions on genuinely relevant matters back to section 137's compel-but-immunise rule.
:::

::: story
Sections 147 and 148 are both about documents, but from opposite ends: section 147 stops a witness's oral account from substituting for the document itself, while section 148 lets a witness be confronted with their own prior written words without ceremony — unless the confrontation is meant to contradict them, in which case fair notice of exactly which passage is being used is required first. Section 149's list of lawful cross-examination questions is deliberately broad — the Act accepts that testing a witness's veracity, identity, and credit sometimes means uncomfortable, even self-incriminating, questions — but the 2023 Act adds something the 1872 Act never had: an express bar on using a complainant's general immoral character or sexual history to prove consent in prosecutions for the enumerated sexual offences, a change that speaks directly to decades of trial practice this project's own honesty rule requires acknowledging as a genuine, not merely renumbered, change.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

> **147.** Any witness may be asked, while under examination, whether any contract, grant or other disposition of property, as to which he is giving evidence, was not contained in a document, and if he says that it was, or if he is about to make any statement as to the contents of any document, which, in the opinion of the Court, ought to be produced, the adverse party may [[!object to such evidence being given until such document is produced]], or until facts have been proved which entitle the party who called the witness to give secondary evidence of it.
>
> *Explanation.* — A witness may give oral evidence of statements made by other persons about the contents of documents if such [[?statements are in themselves relevant facts]].
>
> **148.** A witness may be cross-examined as to previous statements made by him in writing or reduced into writing, and relevant to matters in question, [[?without such writing being shown to him, or being proved]]; but, if it is intended to contradict him by the writing, his attention must, before the writing can be proved, be [[!called to those parts of it which are to be used for the purpose of contradicting him]].
>
> **149.** When a witness is cross-examined, he may, in addition to the questions hereinbefore referred to, be asked any questions which tend—(a) to test his veracity; or (b) to discover who he is and what is his position in life; or (c) to shake his credit, by injuring his character, [[!although the answer to such questions might tend directly or indirectly to criminate him, or might expose or tend directly or indirectly to expose him to a penalty or forfeiture]]:
>
> Provided that in a prosecution for an offence under section 64, section 65, section 66, section 67, section 68, section 69, section 70 or section 71 of the Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita, 2023 or for attempt to commit any such offence, where the question of consent is an issue, [[!it shall not be permissible to adduce evidence or to put questions in the cross-examination of the victim as to the general immoral character, or previous sexual experience, of such victim with any person for proving such consent or the quality of consent]].
>
> **150.** If any such question relates to a matter relevant to the suit or proceeding, the [[!provisions of section 137 shall apply thereto]].

| Text | What it does | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| S. 147's "object... until such document is produced" | Lets the adverse party force production (or proof of secondary-evidence entitlement) before oral testimony about a document's contents proceeds | Stops a party from proving a document's terms by mere say-so when the document itself is available |
| S. 147's Explanation | Preserves oral evidence of statements *about* a document that are independently relevant facts (not offered to prove the document's own contents) | The Act's own illustration — a threat referencing an accusatory letter — shows this is about relevance of the statement itself, not a document-contents proof |
| S. 148's "without such writing being shown... or being proved" | Lets cross-examination on prior written statements proceed freely, without first laying a documentary foundation | Speeds up impeachment cross-examination considerably compared to a stricter "show the document first" rule |
| S. 148's contradiction proviso | Requires fair notice — pointing to the specific passage — before actually using the writing to contradict the witness | Protects the witness from being blindsided by a document sprung on them without any chance to explain |
| S. 149(a)-(c) | Confirms veracity, identity, and credit-shaking questions are lawful even where self-incriminating | Cross-examination's purpose extends beyond the facts in issue to testing the witness as a source |
| S. 149's proviso | Bars sexual-history and general-immoral-character evidence to prove consent, in prosecutions for the enumerated BNS sexual offences or their attempt | A genuine substantive change protecting complainants, not a renumbering — see the concordance section below |
| S. 150's cross-reference | Applies section 137's compel-but-immunise rule wherever the credit-question is on a matter otherwise relevant to the suit | Keeps the self-incrimination trade-off (compelled answer, but no arrest/prosecution based on it) consistent between ordinary examination and credit-testing cross-examination |

::: proviso Section 147's Explanation is about the statement's independent relevance, not a loophole
The Act's own illustration is precise: proving that a person said "you accused me by letter, and I will be revenged" is relevant to show motive for an assault, and can be proved without ever producing the letter — because the statement itself (the threat) is the relevant fact, not the letter's contents. A party cannot use this Explanation to smuggle in a document's actual terms by dressing them up as "something someone said about it."
:::

::: proviso Section 149's proviso protects the complainant, not the accused's right to ask any question
The proviso does not bar all cross-examination of a complainant in these prosecutions — it bars only questions and evidence going to general immoral character or previous sexual experience offered specifically to prove consent or its quality. Cross-examination on the actual facts of the incident, inconsistencies in the complainant's account, or other genuinely relevant matters remains available; what is barred is the specific historical tactic of putting the complainant's sexual history on trial as a proxy for consent.
:::

### In plain English

**Sections 147 and 148 both police the relationship between oral testimony and documents, in opposite directions.** Section 147 stops a witness's words from quietly replacing a document that ought to be produced. Section 148 makes cross-examination on a witness's own prior written statement easy and unceremonious, with one fairness safeguard: if the writing is actually going to be used to contradict the witness, the witness must first be shown which part.

**Sections 149 and 150 define how far cross-examination can lawfully go in testing a witness as a person, not just as a source of facts.** The general rule is generous to the cross-examiner — veracity, identity, and even self-incriminating credit questions are all fair game. The one deliberate, modern exception is the bar on using a sexual-offence complainant's general character or sexual history to argue consent — a rule this Act adds precisely because that historical tactic caused real harm and proved little.

### Test your instinct

::::instinct Scenario 9 — describing a contract without producing it
A witness begins testifying, "The lease said the rent was fifty thousand a month," without the lease ever being produced or its absence explained. Opposing counsel objects. Should the objection succeed?
:::ruling
Yes. Section 147 lets the adverse party object to oral evidence of a document's contents until the document itself is produced, or until facts entitling the party to give secondary evidence are proved. Simply asserting what a document said, without producing it or justifying secondary evidence, is exactly what section 147 exists to stop.
:::
::::

::::instinct Scenario 10 — the surprise letter used to contradict
Counsel cross-examines a witness on a letter the witness wrote years earlier, asking broad questions about its general subject without showing it to the witness, and this is permitted. Counsel then attempts to read out a specific damaging line from the letter to contradict the witness's testimony, without ever having called the witness's attention to that line. Is this proper?
:::ruling
No, not as to the contradiction. Section 148 allows cross-examination on the prior writing without showing it to the witness — that part was fine. But the moment counsel intends to *contradict* the witness using the writing, the witness's attention must first be called to the specific parts to be used for that purpose, before the writing can be proved. Skipping straight to reading out the damaging line without that notice is improper, however permissible the earlier, more general questioning was.
:::
::::

::::instinct Scenario 11 — probing a witness's own criminal exposure to shake credit
In cross-examination, a witness is asked a question that would, if answered honestly, reveal the witness's own past minor dishonesty, offered purely to shake the witness's credit. The witness objects that the question risks self-incrimination. Should the objection succeed?
:::ruling
No. Section 149(c) expressly permits credit-shaking questions "although the answer to such questions might tend directly or indirectly to criminate him." Self-incrimination is not, by itself, a valid objection to a lawful credit-shaking question under this section. Section 150 then supplies the witness's actual protection — if the matter is otherwise relevant to the proceeding, section 137's use-immunity applies to the compelled answer — but the compulsion to answer stands.
:::
::::

::::instinct Scenario 12 — the barred consent-history question
In a prosecution under Bharatiya Nyaya Sanhita section 65, with consent squarely in issue, defence counsel seeks to cross-examine the complainant about her past sexual relationships with other people, arguing it bears on whether she consented on the occasion in question. Should the Court permit this?
:::ruling
No. The proviso to section 149 is squarely on point: in a prosecution under BNS sections 64 to 71 or an attempt at such an offence, where consent is in issue, it is impermissible to adduce evidence or put questions in cross-examination of the victim as to general immoral character or previous sexual experience with any person, for the purpose of proving consent or its quality. Even though such questions might otherwise fall within the broad "credit-shaking" category permitted by section 149's general rule, this specific proviso removes them entirely from what defence counsel may ask on this particular ground.
:::
::::

#### Strategy and drafting
Before leading a witness through a document's contents, always have the document itself, or clear secondary-evidence grounds, ready — an unprepared attempt to describe a document's terms invites an immediate and often fatal objection under section 147. When planning to contradict a witness with their own prior writing, prepare the specific passage in advance and put it to the witness properly rather than risk the entire line of impeachment being disallowed for want of notice. And in any prosecution touching the enumerated sexual offences, flag the section 149 proviso at the earliest possible stage — ideally before trial — so that no line of cross-examination is planned around a tactic the proviso forecloses outright.

## Sections 151 to 155 — the Court's control over credit-shaking questions

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute
Not every credit-shaking question is on a matter relevant to the case. Section 151 gives the Court discretion over whether a witness must answer a question relevant only to credit, not to the matter in issue, and lets the Court draw an adverse inference from a refusal to answer. Section 152 requires the questioner to have reasonable grounds before even asking such a question. Section 153 lets the Court report an advocate who asks such a question without reasonable grounds to the High Court or other professional authority. Section 154 lets the Court forbid indecent or scandalous questions unrelated to facts in issue. And section 155 requires the Court to forbid questions intended merely to insult or annoy, or needlessly offensive in form. Together, these five sections are the Court's toolkit for keeping cross-examination probing without letting it become abusive.
:::

::: story
Section 151's four listed considerations do real work: a question is proper only if the truth of its imputation would seriously affect the Court's view of the witness's credibility on the matter actually testified to; it is improper if the imputation is too remote in time or character to matter much, or if there is a gross disproportion between the imputation's severity and the importance of the witness's evidence. Section 152's reasonable-grounds requirement, illustrated vividly by the Act's own "is he a dacoit?" examples, stops a lawyer from firing off a devastating but baseless accusation purely for its shock value — while confirming that even a single credible tip, properly followed up, can supply the needed reasonable ground.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

> **151.** (1) If any such question relates to a matter not relevant to the suit or proceeding, except in so far as it affects the credit of the witness by injuring his character, the Court shall [[!decide whether or not the witness shall be compelled to answer it]], and may, if it thinks fit, warn the witness that he is not obliged to answer it.
>
> (2) In exercising its discretion, the Court shall have regard to the following considerations, namely:—(a) such questions are proper if [[!the truth of the imputation conveyed by them would seriously affect the opinion of the Court as to the credibility of the witness]] on the matter to which he testifies; (b) such questions are improper if the imputation conveyed relates to [[?matters so remote in time, or of such a character, that the truth of the imputation would not affect, or would affect in a slight degree, the opinion of the Court as to the credibility of the witness]]; (c) such questions are improper if there is a [[?great disproportion between the importance of the imputation made against the witness's character and the importance of his evidence]]; (d) the Court may, if it sees fit, [[?draw, from the witness's refusal to answer, the inference that the answer if given would be unfavourable]].
>
> **152.** No such question as is referred to in section 151 ought to be asked, unless the person asking it has [[!reasonable grounds for thinking that the imputation which it conveys is well-founded]].
>
> **153.** If the Court is of opinion that any such question was asked without reasonable grounds, it may, if it was asked by any advocate, [[!report the circumstances of the case to the High Court or other authority to which such advocate is subject]] in the exercise of his profession.
>
> **154.** The Court may [[!forbid any questions or inquiries which it regards as indecent or scandalous]], although such questions or inquiries may have some bearing on the questions before the Court, unless they relate to facts in issue, or to matters necessary to be known in order to determine whether or not the facts in issue existed.
>
> **155.** The Court shall [[!forbid any question which appears to it to be intended to insult or annoy]], or which, though proper in itself, appears to the Court needlessly offensive in form.

| Text | What it does | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| S. 151(1)'s "shall decide... may... warn" | Gives the Court, not either party, the final call on whether an off-topic credit question must be answered, plus an optional warning to the witness | The witness is not automatically protected — the Court must actively decide, considering the section 151(2) factors |
| S. 151(2)(a)-(c) | Supplies the actual test: seriousness of the credibility impact versus remoteness and proportionality | A structured balancing test, not an open-ended discretion |
| S. 151(2)(d) | Lets the Court draw an adverse inference from a refusal to answer | A real cost to invoking the Court's discretion to excuse an answer — silence is not free |
| S. 152's "reasonable grounds" | Requires the questioner to have a genuine basis before even asking, not just before pressing for an answer | Filters out speculative, purely tactical accusations at the point of asking, not only at the point of ruling |
| S. 153's reporting power | Gives the Court a real disciplinary consequence for baseless questioning by advocates specifically | A meaningful deterrent beyond simply disallowing the question in the moment |
| S. 154's indecent/scandalous bar | Forbids indecent or scandalous inquiries unless tied to facts in issue | Recognises some inquiries are inherently degrading regardless of any tangential relevance |
| S. 155's insult/annoy bar | Mandatory ("shall"), not discretionary, and reaches even a "proper" question asked in a needlessly offensive form | Protects the manner of questioning, not just its substance |

::: proviso Section 152's illustrations set the reasonable-grounds bar at genuine information, not gut instinct
The Act's own four illustrations are instructive: instructions from another advocate that a witness is a "dacoit," or a satisfactorily explained tip from a person in Court, both supply reasonable grounds. Asking the same question of a stranger "at random," with nothing known about them, does not. But unsatisfactory answers to routine questions about a witness's mode of life and means of living can themselves become a reasonable ground for the pointed question that follows. The dividing line is whether *something concrete* — a source, a tip, an unsatisfactory answer — underlies the question, not whether the questioner merely suspects or hopes.
:::

### In plain English

**Five sections, one shared purpose: letting the Court police credit-only questions without banning them outright.** Section 151 is the Court's core discretion, guided by a real balancing test and backed by the threat of an adverse inference for an excused refusal. Section 152 pushes the discipline earlier — a question needs reasonable grounds before it is even asked. Section 153 adds teeth for advocates specifically, through professional reporting. And sections 154-155 protect the witness's dignity directly, forbidding indecent, scandalous, insulting, or needlessly offensive questions regardless of their tactical value.

### Test your instinct

::::instinct Scenario 13 — the reasonable ground from a satisfactorily explained tip
An advocate is told by a bystander in the courtroom that an important witness has a serious credibility problem, and after questioning the bystander directly, receives a specific and plausible explanation for the claim. The advocate then asks the witness a pointed credit-shaking question based on this. Opposing counsel objects that the advocate had no real basis. Is the objection sound?
:::ruling
No. This tracks the Act's own second illustration to section 152 closely: information from a person in Court, followed up and satisfactorily explained on questioning, supplies reasonable grounds for the question. The advocate did not act on a rumour alone — the follow-up and satisfactory explanation is exactly what converts a stray tip into a reasonable ground.
:::
::::

::::instinct Scenario 14 — the random, baseless accusation
An advocate, knowing nothing whatsoever about a witness, asks at random whether the witness has ever committed fraud, purely to see if it rattles the witness. Is this a proper question under section 152?
:::ruling
No. The Act's own third illustration to section 152 addresses this exact scenario: a witness of whom nothing whatever is known, asked at random, has no reasonable grounds behind the question. The advocate cannot justify the question after the fact by hoping it happens to be true — reasonable grounds must exist before the question is asked, and speculation is not enough.
:::
::::

::::instinct Scenario 15 — the disproportionate imputation against an important witness
A witness gives crucial, central testimony on the main issue in the case. Opposing counsel seeks to ask a credit-shaking question about a minor, decades-old social embarrassment that has no real bearing on honesty. Should the Court compel an answer?
:::ruling
No, and this is precisely what section 151(2)(c) is for: such questions are improper where there is a great disproportion between the importance of the imputation made against the witness's character and the importance of his evidence. A trivial, ancient embarrassment weighed against genuinely crucial testimony tips the balance toward disallowing the question, even if the imputation is technically true.
:::
::::

::::instinct Scenario 16 — the witness who stays silent and pays for it
A witness is excused by the Court from answering a credit-only question after being warned he need not answer. The witness declines to answer. The opposing side argues the Court should now infer, against the witness, that a truthful answer would have been unfavourable. Is that a legitimate inference for the Court to draw?
:::ruling
Yes. Section 151(2)(d) expressly permits the Court, if it sees fit, to draw from a witness's refusal to answer the inference that the answer, if given, would have been unfavourable. Being excused from answering is not the same as being shielded from every consequence of the refusal — the witness's silence carries a real cost even when the Court has properly exercised its discretion to allow that silence.
:::

#### Strategy and drafting
Before asking any credit-only question with no direct bearing on the facts in issue, document the concrete source of the belief that the imputation is well-founded — a tip, a prior inconsistent record, or direct knowledge — so the reasonable-grounds requirement can be defended if challenged on the spot. When defending a witness against such a question, object early and specifically, naming which of sections 151 through 155 is engaged, rather than a general "objection, relevance" that fails to trigger the Court's more exacting reasonable-grounds and proportionality analysis. And where a question is baseless and clearly designed only to wound, consider inviting the Court to exercise its section 153 reporting power — a step advocates should use sparingly, but which exists precisely to deter this kind of tactic.

## Chapter X part II consolidation — was this question about a document, or about the witness's character?

1. **Is the witness's oral evidence effectively substituting for a document that ought to be produced?** Section 147 stops that substitution; it does not stop independently relevant statements *about* a document.
2. **Is a prior written statement being used merely to test the witness, or to actually contradict them?** Only the latter triggers section 148's fair-notice requirement.
3. **Does the question fall within section 149's lawful categories, and — critically — is this a prosecution where the section 149 proviso removes sexual-history and character evidence from the table entirely?**
4. **If the question touches only credit and not the matter in issue, has the questioner cleared section 152's reasonable-grounds threshold before asking, and does the question survive sections 151 and 154-155's balancing and decency filters?**
