# Chapter IX part I — Of Witnesses: competency and the privilege against compelled answers (ss. 124-131)

BSA Chapter IX opens Part III's final stretch on the mechanics of proof: not what facts are relevant, or how documents are proved, but who may give evidence at all, and what a witness can be forced to say. This first part covers two things: who counts as competent to testify (ss. 124-126), and a family of five narrow privileges that let a witness — often not even a party — refuse to answer certain questions altogether (ss. 127-131).

## Sections 124 to 126 — who may testify

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute
A party wants to call a six-year-old child as an eyewitness to an accident. The opposing side objects: surely a child cannot testify. The law's answer is not an age cut-off at all — it is a functional test. The child (or any other witness — the very old, the unwell, anyone whose capacity is in question) is competent so long as the Court considers them able to understand the questions put to them and to give rational answers. A witness who cannot speak is not excluded either — they may testify by writing or by signs, and if they cannot communicate verbally at all, the Court must bring in an interpreter or special educator and videograph the statement. And in most disputes, a party's own spouse — often the person who knows the most about what actually happened at home — is a fully competent witness too.
:::

::: story
Competency answers one question only: can this person meaningfully take part in giving evidence at all? It has nothing to do with how much weight their evidence should get once given — that is a separate question for the trier of fact. Section 124 refuses to disqualify entire categories of people (children, the elderly, the unwell) by a blunt rule; it insists on an individual, functional assessment instead. Sections 125 and 126 then close two gaps that a rigid, speech-only, party-and-nobody-else model of "witness" would otherwise leave open.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

> **124.** All persons shall be competent to testify unless the Court [[!considers that they are prevented from understanding the questions put to them, or from giving rational answers to those questions]], by tender years, extreme old age, disease, whether of body or mind, or any other cause of the same kind.
>
> *Explanation.* — A person of unsound mind [[?is not incompetent to testify]], unless he is prevented by his unsoundness of mind from understanding the questions put to him and giving rational answers to them.
>
> **125.** A witness who is unable to speak [[?may give his evidence in any other manner in which he can make it intelligible]], as by writing or by signs; but such writing must be written and the signs made in open Court and evidence so given shall be deemed to be oral evidence:
>
> Provided that if the witness is unable to communicate verbally, the Court [[!shall take the assistance of an interpreter or a special educator]] in recording the statement, and such statement [[!shall be videographed]].
>
> **126.** (1) In all civil proceedings the parties to the suit, and the husband or wife of any party to the suit, [[!shall be competent witnesses]].
>
> (2) In criminal proceedings against any person, the husband or wife of such person, respectively, [[!shall be a competent witness]].

| Text | What it does | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| "prevented from understanding... or from giving rational answers" | States the entire competency test as one functional inquiry, not a checklist of disqualifying categories | Tender years, extreme old age, and disease are only *examples* of causes that might trigger the test — none of them disqualifies automatically |
| "or any other cause of the same kind" | Keeps the list of possible causes open-ended, by the same logic that runs elsewhere in the Act | A cause not named — trauma, intoxication at the time of testifying, a communication disorder — can still trigger the same inquiry |
| Explanation to s. 124 (unsoundness of mind) | Expressly rejects a blanket "unsound mind = incompetent" rule | Mental illness disqualifies only if it actually prevents understanding and rational answering on the facts, not as a status label |
| S. 125's "deemed to be oral evidence" | Writing or signs used by a witness unable to speak are treated exactly like spoken testimony, not as documentary evidence | Keeps such testimony inside the oral-evidence and cross-examination regime rather than routing it through Chapter V's document rules |
| S. 125 proviso (interpreter/special educator, videography) | Makes both the assistance and the video record mandatory, not discretionary, wherever verbal communication is not possible | A structured safeguard — this is new machinery worth flagging, since the interpreter/special-educator-and-videography combination is more detailed than a bare "may use signs" rule would need to be |
| S. 126(1)-(2) (spousal competency) | Makes a party's own spouse a competent witness in civil proceedings, and the accused's spouse a competent witness in criminal proceedings against that accused | Removes any doubt that marriage itself disqualifies a spouse from testifying for or against the other — competency and privilege are two different things, and s. 128 below is where the real restriction on marital communications lives |

::: proviso Section 125's safeguard is mandatory, not a discretionary courtesy
The proviso does not leave it to the Court's convenience whether to arrange an interpreter or special educator, or whether to record the statement on video, once a witness cannot communicate verbally. Both the assistance and the videography are cast in "shall" language. A trial that skips either step for a witness who genuinely cannot communicate verbally has not merely been less careful — it has failed to follow the section.
:::

### In plain English

**Competency is about capacity, not category.** Nobody is disqualified simply for being very young, very old, unwell, or married to a party — the only question is whether this particular person, on this particular day, can understand questions and answer them rationally. A witness who cannot speak is not shut out either: writing, signs, or (where verbal communication is impossible altogether) an interpreter or special educator with a videographed record, all count as valid ways of giving oral evidence.

Do not confuse competency with privilege. A spouse is fully competent to testify under section 126 — but section 128, just below, may still stop them from being compelled to reveal what was actually said between husband and wife during the marriage. Being allowed to take the stand and being forced to answer every question on it are two different protections.

### Test your instinct

::::instinct Scenario 1 — the seven-year-old eyewitness
A seven-year-old is the only eyewitness to a hit-and-run accident. The defence argues a child of that age cannot possibly be a competent witness. Is the defence right?
:::ruling
Not automatically. Section 124 asks only whether the Court considers this child prevented from understanding the questions put to her and from giving rational answers — tender years is merely one example of a cause that might trigger that finding, not an automatic disqualification. If the Court, on examining the child, finds she can understand and answer rationally, she is a competent witness. The defence must show the specific incapacity, not merely point to the child's age.
:::
::::

::::instinct Scenario 2 — the witness with a diagnosed mental illness
A witness has a long-documented diagnosis of a mood disorder. The opposing party argues this makes him incompetent to testify under section 124. Is that enough?
:::ruling
No. The Explanation to section 124 expressly states that a person of unsound mind is not incompetent to testify unless the unsoundness actually prevents him from understanding the questions and giving rational answers. A diagnosis alone, without a showing that it affects this witness's ability to understand and answer rationally at the relevant time, does not satisfy the test. The inquiry must be functional and specific, not a label applied wholesale.
:::
::::

::::instinct Scenario 3 — the witness who can only communicate through an interpreter
A key witness in a criminal trial has a speech and hearing disability and can communicate only through a sign-language interpreter. The trial court simply lets the interpreter translate in the ordinary course, without arranging a special educator or recording video. Is this compliant with section 125?
:::ruling
No — at least not fully. Section 125's proviso is written in mandatory terms: where a witness is unable to communicate verbally, the Court *shall* take the assistance of an interpreter or a special educator in recording the statement, and the statement *shall* be videographed. Using an interpreter alone, without ensuring the videography requirement is also met, falls short of what the section demands. This is exactly the kind of overlooked procedural safeguard a defence or prosecution team should flag immediately, not raise for the first time on appeal.
:::
::::

::::instinct Scenario 4 — the wife called against her husband
A wife is called by the prosecution to testify against her husband in a criminal trial. The defence argues she cannot be compelled to testify at all because she is his spouse. Is the defence right about competency?
:::ruling
No, not as a matter of competency. Section 126(2) makes the spouse of an accused person a competent witness in criminal proceedings against that person — marriage does not disqualify her from taking the stand. What the defence may have in mind is a different protection: section 128's privilege over marital *communications*, which could stop her being compelled to disclose specific things her husband said to her during the marriage. But that is a privilege over particular questions, not a bar on her competency to testify at all.
:::
::::

### Practitioner HUD — operational realities

#### The winning ratio
*Rameshwar v. State of Rajasthan*, AIR 1952 SC 54. A child witness's evidence was challenged for want of a competency inquiry and administration of oath. The Supreme Court held that the competency of a child witness is a matter for the trial Court's own determination on examining the child, and that the real safeguard against unreliable child testimony is not a rigid age bar but caution in evaluating the evidence and, generally, looking for corroboration — not exclusion at the threshold. **Practitioner takeaway**: plead affirmatively that the trial Court conducted (or should conduct) a preliminary competency examination of a child or otherwise vulnerable witness, and argue reliability through corroboration at the appreciation stage, not through a blanket incompetency objection that the statute itself does not support.

#### Sword and shield
| | As a sword (calling the witness) | As a shield (resisting the witness) |
|---|---|---|
| S. 124 | Insist on a case-specific competency finding rather than accepting a blanket "too young / too old / too unwell" objection | Where genuine incapacity exists, seek a preliminary competency examination on the record before the witness testifies on the merits |
| S. 125 | Arrange the interpreter or special educator and videography early, so an unable-to-communicate-verbally witness's evidence is not vulnerable to a later procedural challenge | Object promptly, at the time the evidence is recorded, if the mandatory interpreter/special-educator-and-videography safeguard is skipped |
| S. 126 | Call a party's spouse without fear of a bare competency objection | Redirect any real objection toward section 128's marital-communications privilege, not competency, where the concern is actually about what was said in confidence |

#### The limitation clock
None of its own. Competency is assessed at the time the witness is examined, within whatever proceeding is otherwise properly before the Court, and carries no independent limitation period.

#### Interlocking matrix
| This provision | Interlocks with | Why |
|---|---|---|
| S. 124 (general competency) | This Act, s. 55 (directness rule, Chapter IV) | A witness found competent under section 124 must still satisfy section 55's directness requirement — competency and directness are two separate gates the same witness's evidence must clear |
| S. 125 (unable to communicate verbally) | Bharatiya Nagarik Suraksha Sanhita, 2023 (statement-recording and videography provisions generally) | The mandatory videography requirement here sits alongside BNSS's own broader push toward videographed recording of statements and proceedings — a practitioner should check both regimes together rather than treating section 125 as a standalone rule |
| S. 126 (spousal competency) | This Act, s. 128 below (marital communications privilege) | Competency to testify and privilege over specific communications are adjacent but distinct protections operating on the same witness |

#### The authorities
No further authority beyond the promoted ratio is confidently recalled for this cluster with the certainty this project's citation rule requires.

#### Strategy and drafting
When a witness's competency is genuinely in doubt — a young child, an elderly witness, someone with a cognitive or psychiatric condition — ask for a preliminary competency examination on the record before the witness testifies on the merits, so the finding (and its basis) survives appeal. When a witness cannot communicate verbally, raise the section 125 safeguards proactively rather than waiting for the opposing side to skip them — build a clean record with an interpreter or special educator and a video recording from the outset. And when the real concern about a spouse-witness is confidentiality rather than capacity, plead it as a section 128 privilege objection, not a section 126 competency objection — the two provisions do different jobs and a misdirected objection is easily overruled.

## Sections 127 to 131 — the privilege against answering certain questions

### The dispute this solves

::: oneminute
A litigant wants to question a sitting Magistrate about what happened during an earlier proceeding before him, or wants a witness to reveal a private letter exchanged with their spouse, or wants a government officer to produce a classified internal file, or wants to know precisely when the police first learned of an offence. In each of these situations, the witness may well be fully competent, and the information may well be relevant — but the law still says no, absent something more. Sections 127 to 131 gather five narrow, purpose-built privileges: a Judge's or Magistrate's own judicial conduct, communications between married persons, unpublished State affairs, official communications made in confidence, and the timing of information about an offence reaching an investigating or revenue officer. Each protects a specific relationship or institutional interest that the law has decided is worth shielding from ordinary compulsion.
:::

::: story
These five sections are not one rule wearing different hats — they are five separate policy judgments, each answering a different question about when institutional or relational trust should outweigh a litigant's ordinary right to compel an answer. A Judge should not be dragged into cross-examination about every ruling he has made; a marriage should not become discoverable through its private correspondence; the State should be able to keep some affairs unpublished; officials should be able to receive confidences without fear of automatic disclosure; and an investigation's internal timeline should not always have to be laid bare. Reading them together is useful for spotting the pattern, but each has its own precise trigger and its own precise limit — treat them as five locks, not one.
:::

### The Act, decompiled

> **127.** No Judge or Magistrate shall, except upon the special order of some Court to which he is subordinate, [[!be compelled to answer any question as to his own conduct in Court]] as such Judge or Magistrate, or as to anything which came to his knowledge in Court as such Judge or Magistrate; but he [[?may be examined as to other matters which occurred in his presence whilst he was so acting]].
>
> **128.** No person who is or has been married, shall [[!be compelled to disclose any communication made to him during marriage]] by any person to whom he is or has been married; nor shall he [[!be permitted to disclose any such communication]], unless the person who made it, or his representative in interest, consents, except in suits between married persons, or proceedings in which one married person is prosecuted for any crime committed against the other.
>
> **129.** No one shall be permitted to give any evidence derived from unpublished official records relating to any affairs of State, [[!except with the permission of the officer at the head of the department concerned]], who shall give or withhold such permission as he thinks fit.
>
> **130.** No public officer shall [[!be compelled to disclose communications made to him in official confidence]], when he considers that the public interests would suffer by the disclosure.
>
> **131.** No Magistrate or police officer shall [[!be compelled to say when he got any information as to the commission of any offence]], and no revenue officer shall be compelled to say when he got any information as to the commission of any offence against the public revenue.
>
> *Explanation.* — "revenue officer" means any officer employed in or about the business of any branch of the public revenue.

| Text | What it does | Why it matters |
|---|---|---|
| S. 127's "except upon the special order of some Court to which he is subordinate" | The Judge/Magistrate privilege is not absolute — a superior Court can order the answer | The privilege protects judicial independence from routine harassment, not from genuine superior-court scrutiny |
| S. 127's second limb ("other matters which occurred in his presence") | Draws a line between what the Judge did or learned *as* Judge, and what merely happened in front of him while on the bench | A Judge can still be examined as an ordinary percipient witness to events, just not interrogated about his own judicial conduct or knowledge |
| S. 128's dual bar (compelled disclosure *and* permitted disclosure) | Protects marital communications from both sides — the spouse cannot be forced to tell, and cannot even volunteer to tell, without the consent of the spouse who made the communication (or that spouse's representative) | This is a genuine privilege belonging to the communicating spouse, not merely a compellability rule the listening spouse can waive on their own |
| S. 128's carve-outs (suits between married persons; a crime by one against the other) | Removes the privilege exactly where it would otherwise shield the very relationship the litigation is about | A spouse suing or prosecuting the other cannot be blocked by the same privilege that exists to protect the marriage in ordinary third-party litigation |
| S. 129's "except with the permission of the officer at the head of the department" | Puts the release decision entirely in the hands of the relevant department head, "as he thinks fit" | This is a discretionary institutional gatekeeper, not a judicial balancing test — the Court does not independently weigh whether disclosure should occur |
| S. 130's "when he considers that the public interests would suffer" | The public officer's own subjective assessment of public interest is what triggers the privilege | Unlike section 129, section 130's judgment call sits with the officer receiving the confidence, not with a department head or the Court |
| S. 131's narrow subject-matter (only the *timing* of information) | Protects only *when* information about an offence was received, not the information itself or its source | A very specific investigative-integrity protection — it does not create a general privilege over what an investigating or revenue officer knows |

::: proviso Section 128 protects the communication, not the underlying fact
If a fact was independently observed by the listening spouse — rather than communicated in words — section 128 does not apply to it at all; the section is confined to communications *made* during the marriage, not to everything one spouse happens to know about the other. Do not over-read the privilege into a bar on all spousal testimony about the marriage generally.
:::

::: proviso Sections 129 and 130 hand the decision to the executive, not the Court
Both sections put the disclosure decision in the hands of an official, not a judge weighing competing interests case by case. A litigant frustrated by a refusal under either section does not have a direct route to have a Court simply override that refusal on the merits — the remedy, if any, lies in challenging the official's exercise of that discretion on its own terms, not in treating the Court as a first-instance decision-maker on whether the material should come out.
:::

### In plain English

**These five sections are exceptions carved out of the ordinary rule that a competent witness must answer relevant questions.** Each protects a different relationship or institutional interest: judicial independence (s. 127), the privacy of marriage (s. 128), the confidentiality of State affairs (s. 129), official confidences generally (s. 130), and the timing of an investigation's own knowledge (s. 131). None of them is a general immunity — each is triggered only by its own specific category of question, and most come with their own carve-out (a superior Court's special order, the communicating spouse's consent, the department head's permission, the officer's own public-interest judgment).

The most important distinction to hold onto is between section 128, which is a true dual-sided privilege belonging to the spouse who made the communication, and sections 129-130, which are discretionary gatekeeping powers exercised by an official rather than the Court. Do not argue a section 129 or 130 refusal as though it were a privilege the witness personally holds and can waive — the decision belongs to someone else entirely.

### Test your instinct

::::instinct Scenario 5 — cross-examining the trial judge on appeal
On appeal, a party wants to call the trial Judge as a witness to explain why he admitted a particular piece of evidence during the original trial. Can the trial Judge be compelled to answer?
:::ruling
Not without more. Section 127 protects a Judge from being compelled to answer questions about his own conduct in Court, or knowledge acquired there, in his judicial capacity — exactly the kind of question this scenario poses. The privilege can only be lifted by the special order of a Court to which that Judge is subordinate. A party cannot simply subpoena the trial Judge to explain his rulings; the appellate route for challenging a ruling is appeal on the record, not cross-examination of the judge who made it.
:::
::::

::::instinct Scenario 6 — the love letters in a defamation suit
In a defamation suit between two strangers to each other's marriages, one party wants to introduce private letters written by the opposing party's spouse to that party during their marriage, to show a pattern of dishonesty. The spouse who wrote the letters has not consented to their disclosure. Can they be introduced?
:::ruling
No. Section 128 bars not only compelled disclosure but also *permitted* disclosure of a marital communication without the consent of the spouse who made it — and this defamation suit is not a suit between the married persons themselves, nor a prosecution for a crime by one against the other, so neither carve-out applies. The genuinely relevant-seeming letters remain privileged unless the writing spouse consents. This is a case where a real, non-manufactured piece of relevant evidence is kept out entirely by design.
:::
::::

::::instinct Scenario 7 — the officer who will not say when he learned of the crime
A defence team wants to argue that an investigation was manipulated by asking the investigating officer, under oath, exactly when he first received information about the alleged offence, hoping to expose a suspicious delay. The officer refuses to answer, citing section 131. Is the refusal valid?
:::ruling
Yes, as far as it goes. Section 131 specifically protects a Magistrate or police officer from being compelled to say *when* he received information as to the commission of an offence. The officer may validly decline to answer that specific question. This does not mean the underlying delay is unprovable by other means — timestamps, other witnesses, or documentary records may still establish it — but the direct route of compelling the officer's own testimony on timing is closed by this section.
:::
::::

#### The winning ratio
No single authority is promoted for this cluster. Each of the five privileges is narrow, textual, and purpose-specific enough that the sections' own wording, rather than a particular leading judgment, does the analytical work; no specific citation for any of the five is held with the certainty this project's rule requires.

#### Sword and shield
| | As a sword (seeking disclosure) | As a shield (resisting disclosure) |
|---|---|---|
| S. 127 | Seek the special order of a superior Court where a Judge's own conduct genuinely must be examined, rather than attempting direct compulsion | Object immediately to any attempt to question a Judge or Magistrate about judicial conduct without that special order |
| S. 128 | Seek the consent of the communicating spouse, or rely on the between-spouses or crime-against-the-other carve-out, before pursuing marital communications | Invoke the dual bar (compelled and voluntary) wherever a marital communication is sought without the maker's consent |
| S. 129 | Apply formally to the department head for permission, rather than seeking a court order compelling State-affairs material directly | Note that a court is not the first-instance decision-maker here — a refusal is the department head's discretionary call |
| S. 130 | Press the officer (or, where relevant, challenge the basis of) his own public-interest assessment if it appears unreasonable on the facts | Rely on the officer's own considered public-interest judgment as the operative trigger, not a court's independent balancing |
| S. 131 | Prove timing of an investigation's knowledge through other admissible means — documents, other witnesses — where the officer's own testimony is unavailable | Decline to answer the specific timing question, while remaining answerable on the substance of what was investigated |

#### The limitation clock
None of its own. Each privilege is asserted at the point a question is put or material is sought within an existing proceeding, and carries no independent limitation period.

#### Strategy and drafting
Match the objection to the right section — a section 127 objection to questioning a judge is entirely different from a section 128 objection to a marital communication, and conflating them invites an easy rebuttal. Where section 128 is genuinely in play, seek the communicating spouse's consent early rather than litigating the privilege at trial; where sections 129 or 130 block a document, make the formal application to the relevant officer on the record so that any later challenge to the refusal has something concrete to attack. And where section 131 blocks the direct question about timing, plan an alternative documentary or circumstantial route to the same point well before trial — do not discover the gap for the first time while the witness is on the stand.

## Chapter IX part I consolidation — is this witness competent, and is this question privileged?

1. **Is the proposed witness competent under section 124, 125, or 126?** Capacity is assessed functionally and individually — no category of person is disqualified merely by age, health, communication method, or marriage to a party.
2. **If the witness cannot communicate verbally at all, have the mandatory interpreter/special-educator-and-videography safeguards under section 125 actually been followed?** This is a "shall," not a discretionary courtesy.
3. **Even if the witness is competent, does one of the five sections 127-131 privileges block this particular question?** Each is narrow and purpose-specific — a Judge's judicial conduct, a marital communication, State affairs, official confidence, or the timing of an offence's discovery.
4. **Where a privilege applies, does one of its own carve-outs remove it on these facts?** A superior Court's special order, the communicating spouse's consent, a suit between spouses, or a crime by one spouse against the other can each strip the privilege back off.
